## A. Alves dos Reis, Portugal 1925

**Proposed title:** He Never Faked a Single Banknote
Alternates: (1) The Bank's Own Printer Made His Money (2) A Con Man Ordered Real Money From the Bank's Printer

**Premise (one sentence):** A man who got out of jail on a technicality realised he did not need to forge money, only paperwork, and the Bank of Portugal's own printer in London then printed him real banknotes the bank could not tell from its own.

**Opening hook (narration, ~12 s):**
"In 1925 a man in Lisbon ordered one hundred million escudos from the Bank of Portugal's own printer. The bank never ordered it. The printer never asked. And the notes were perfect, because they were real."

**Central conflict:** One man with a fake diploma against a national currency. Stakes: roughly 1% of Portugal's economy (use "about 1%, by one historian's estimate") and, later, the Republic's credibility. Clock: he must make the notes legal before someone checks the serial numbers.

**Escalation steps:**
1. Fake Oxford diploma, spent 54 days in jail (arrested 1924, freed on a technicality). Reportedly hatched the plan there. (Ambaca railway: one line max.)
2. Forged contract, forged bank signatures, stamped and certified by foreign diplomats.
3. Waterlow in London, already printing the real 500-escudo notes, prints 100 million escudos' worth, then more.
4. Suitcases of notes handed over at London's Ritz (attribute: "reportedly", Spink only).
5. He launders them into a private bank, Banco de Angola e Metrópole.
6. He tries to buy the Bank of Portugal itself (about 10,000 shares of the tens of thousands he needed) so the fraud becomes legal after the fact.

**Biggest twist:** He is caught because the notes are too real: the bank had already issued notes with the same serial numbers. A suspicious money-changer's employee, then a hunt through the numbers, then a newspaper (O Século). Second twist: the court made the printer pay.

**Ending / payoff:** The bank honoured every 500-escudo note, real and forged (over 200,000 forged notes exchanged, Dec 1925). He was arrested within days; sentenced in 1930 to "20 years (some accounts split this into prison and exile)"; released 1945, died 1955. The House of Lords made Waterlow pay about GBP 600,000; the man who had approved it, Sir William Waterlow (former Lord Mayor of London, per Wikipedia, not independently verified), had been removed as chairman and died before the final judgment. Close on the loop: "He faked a signature. The bank paid for the real thing." Coup link: one hedged line ("wrecked the Republic's credibility months before the army took over; historians argue about how much").

**Thumbnail concept:** Focal image: two identical banknotes side by side, mascot in the middle pointing at both with a flat confused eyebrow. Text: "BOTH REAL" (2 words; adds to the title, does not repeat it). Mobile read: two matching rectangles and a face are the whole picture; the contrast "identical, yet one is a crime" lands without text. Colours: green/cream notes on dark navy, scarf the only yellow.

**Why it could outperform:** Curiosity: "how can a banknote be both real and stolen?" is a contradiction the viewer must resolve. Emotion: schadenfreude at institutions (bank and printer are the fools), plus admiration for a clean idea. Start state (fake paperwork, a jail cell) -> end state (the bank paying for real money it never ordered). Price tag in scene one (100 million escudos). Universal question: "could someone do that to a bank?"

**Evidence:** Verified: Wikipedia, Spink Insider, Casemine summary of Banco de Portugal v Waterlow [1932] AC 452, BHS Portugal (Bull excerpt), Amusing Planet, PMG (contains an error, ignore). Limitations: Bloom's book, full House of Lords text, Bull and Armstrong-Fleming, Wigan and Portuguese sources NOT opened. Dates conflict (Marang meeting, arrest timing), so use "late 1924" and "within days". Single-source: Ritz suitcases, camphor/lemon "aged" notes, "died a pauper".

**Originality / saturation:** Moderate. Hunters found 3-4 English videos ("Ocean's Portugal...", "The Man Who Nearly Destroyed Portugal's Economy"); H3 found nothing above ~1.2K in its query; no view counts verified. A film adaptation is reportedly in development (May 2026, unverified), so interest may rise. Fresh angle: "he faked the paperwork, not the money" and the printer-pays ending, told as a 5-minute loop. The framing is not new to a viewer who has seen the existing videos.

**Stickman visualisation:** Mascot as a hapless bank clerk holding two identical notes; as the Waterlow printer shrugging while a press runs; a pile of suitcases in a hotel lobby; a serial-number magnifying-glass scene; a ledger with a giant "PAID" stamp at the end. Clean, repeatable props (notes, stamps, seals).

**5-min fit:** Tight. Cut: Ambaca railway to one clause, the second-order detail to one line, shares and bank-governor arrest, accomplice debate, Salazar to one hedged sentence, camphor aging. Keep: forged contract, real printer, serial numbers, bank paying everything, printer paying.

**Honesty risks:** "Printed 100 million" is only the first order (say "ordered 100 million, then more"). Do not say Waterlow was ruined (it survived until a 1961 takeover). No "Dutch tip". Do not say the coup was caused by it. Say the notes were "real printing, unauthorised", never "real money" without the qualifier somewhere in the video. Avoid precise sentence length. "Reportedly hatched it in jail."

**Weakest point:** It is the most-covered of the four on YouTube, the middle (laundering, shares, bank takeover) is abstract finance with few visual beats, and the strongest twist (duplicate serials) is a bureaucratic detail that can feel anticlimactic after the great opening. The "caught by a teller's tip" mechanism is less satisfying than the title promises.

## B. Thérèse Humbert's sealed safe, France

**Proposed title:** The Safe Nobody Was Allowed to Open
Alternates: (1) Paris Lent Her Fortunes Against One Locked Safe (2) She Borrowed Against a Safe for Twenty Years

**Premise:** A peasant's daughter from near Toulouse borrowed tens of millions of francs from Paris's bankers for about twenty years, all against a sealed safe that she said held a dead American millionaire's fortune.

**Opening hook (narration):**
"Paris, 1902. According to her biographer, ten thousand people crowd an avenue to watch locksmiths break open one safe. The woman who owns it has borrowed against what's inside for twenty years. Tens of millions of francs. Today, someone finally gets to look."

**Central conflict:** One woman against every banker in Paris, and against time: the lie must outlast the lenders' patience. Stakes: tens of millions of francs (hedge: "by one account about 50 million, by another nearly 100"), small savers' money. Clock: a lawsuit that can never end, until it does.

**Escalation steps:**
1. She says she revived a dying American millionaire on a train in 1879 and he left her his fortune (she said; the Crawfords never existed).
2. The will supposedly kept the money sealed in a safe until a lawsuit ended; two "Crawford nephews" contested it (her own brothers played the villains).
3. She marries into a politically powerful family (father-in-law a senior politician, at one point Minister of Justice); his name opens doors, not necessarily his involvement.
4. Bankers lend for ~20 years; she pays old lenders with new ones, including a fake annuity fund taking in tens of millions from small savers.
5. A newspaper (Le Matin, 1883) doubts her; the courts rule the safe stays hers.
6. 1901: creditors sue; 1902: the court orders the safe opened.

**Biggest twist:** The safe holds almost nothing. Best-known version: a coin, an old newspaper and a button (accounts differ). And the opposing family she was "fighting" was her own.

**Ending / payoff:** The Humberts fled; arrested in Madrid in December 1902; five years each for Thérèse and Frédéric, 3 and 2 for the brothers. What became of her is unclear; she seems to have vanished (avoid the Chicago-1918 myth). Loop: twenty years of a locked door, and nobody looked until the end.

**Thumbnail concept:** Focal image: a huge iron safe with red wax seals, mascot in a bowler hat leaning on it with a hopeful-then-worried face. Text: "OPEN IT" (2 words) or "20 YEARS". Mobile read: one big black rectangle and a face; the viewer's own curiosity fills the missing half ("what's inside?"). Palette: black safe, wax-red seals, pale cream background, scarf the only yellow. Do not show the empty-safe payoff on the thumbnail.

**Why it could outperform:** Curiosity: a single concrete object and an unanswerable question (what is in the safe?), the "mystery box" mechanism. Emotion: dread-comedy as the lie grows, then satisfaction at the reveal. Universal question: "why didn't anyone just look?" Start (sealed safe, trusted lender) -> end (an almost empty safe). Price tag: tens of millions of francs, plus a 20-year clock.

**Evidence:** Verified/supported: Wikipedia lead, Hilary Spurling's own essay (Times Higher Education, 1999), Evangeline Holland (Edwardian Promenade; cites Spurling and B.F. Martin), Criminocorpus record. Limitations: Spurling's book itself, Atlas Obscura, Gallica/French press, NYT 1903, contemporary 1902 reports NOT opened. Nearly all detail traces to Spurling. Not found: exact date of opening, a firm debt total, an independent record of the safe's contents, number of ruined investors.

**Originality / saturation:** Low in English (H1: French videos only, one German). Fresh angle: the mystery-box framing, told with the viewer as the creditor who is never allowed to look. Caveat: no English YouTube test exists, so we are not proven to have audience demand either. The tradeoff is "low competition, unproven interest".

**Stickman visualisation:** Mascot as a creditor lining up at the safe; as a locksmith; as a polite banker handing over sacks of francs; crowd of dots outside; the safe drawn as an ever-larger character; a "Crawford nephew" figure in a moustache disguise (cheeky, a brother). Strong repeatable prop: the wax seal.

**5-min fit:** Fits well. Cut: Matisse in-laws, banker suicide (single-source, dark), Empress Eugenie claims (speculation), the "1302 Broadway" anecdote (unverified), Chicago death myth. Keep: train lie, safe, nephews, 20 years, opening, flight.

**Honesty risks:** Safe contents disputed: say "almost nothing; accounts differ". Total debt soft. "She sued herself" only as an explicit joke, framed as "in effect she was in a lawsuit against her own family", never as fact. Say "the brothers played the nephews", not "appeared in court as" unless confirmed. Her father-in-law's role is unresolved. The Crawford train meeting is "she said".

**Weakest point:** The reveal is the safe opening, which is spoiled by the title's own subject if the viewer guesses "nothing" in the first ten seconds; the 15-year middle is a repeat of "she borrows again", and the pyramid layer is single-blog sourced. The pay-off depends on contents we must hedge, which is awkward for a story whose whole twist is "what was inside".

## C. Kiska 1943: "Battle of the Pips"

**Proposed title:** 34,000 Soldiers Invaded an Island. Nobody Was There.
Alternates: (1) The Navy Fired 518 Shells at Nothing (2) The Biggest Battle in Alaska Had No Enemy

**Premise:** The Allies spent weeks pounding a Japanese-held island in Alaska, landed more than 34,000 men in a fog, and found only a few stray dogs, because more than 5,000 Japanese had slipped away in about 50 minutes, eighteen days earlier.

**Opening hook:**
"August 1943. Thirty-four thousand soldiers hit the beach on an island off Alaska. They have been told to expect nine thousand enemy troops. They're met by a few stray dogs. And somehow, people still die."

(The "nine thousand" expectation is single-source, Boomhower: either attribute "planners reportedly expected" or cut.)

**Central conflict:** An enormous force against an enemy that is not there; a fog that makes everyone the enemy. Stakes: lives, and the embarrassment. Clock: the invasion fleet is already steaming in.

**Escalation steps:**
1. Japan occupied Kiska and Attu in June 1942; Attu retaken in May 1943 at heavy cost.
2. Night of 26-27 July: US battleships fire 518 big shells at radar contacts. Nobody knows what the "pips" were (birds, echoes, who knows) and no Japanese warships were near.
3. 28 July: Japanese cruisers and destroyers take off more than 5,000 men in about 50 minutes under fog; the blockade had thinned (partly for refuelling). Reportedly they had tried and aborted once before (attribute: Japanese-side blog).
4. For about two and a half weeks the Allies keep bombing and shelling an empty island.
5. 15-16 August: 34,000+ land (including Canadians); nobody is there. Fog, nerves, booby traps, mines.
6. USS Abner Read hits a mine, about 70 die (at sea, 17-18 Aug).

**Biggest twist:** The Japanese left right under the blockade's nose, and the Americans kept shooting at an empty island for 18 days; the Pips were a separate event two days before the escape, and nobody ever worked out what the Navy was firing at.

**Ending / payoff:** About 300 casualties by most counts (range ~300 to over 500); roughly 25-32 killed by friendly fire according to different accounts. Sherrod's friendly-fire report was cut by censors (he agreed at the time). Closing line (loop): "The only thing the invasion captured was a few stray dogs and a lot of mistakes." Careful on tone: the joke is aimed at the planning and the radar, not the dead.

**Thumbnail concept:** Focal image: mascot in a helmet, rifle aimed at a tiny wagging dog in thick fog on an empty beach. Text: "NOBODY HOME" or "34,000 vs ?" (keep it to "VS NOBODY"). Mobile read: one huge soldier-sized mascot against one tiny dog; contrast in scale. Colours: grey fog, dark helmet, scarf the only yellow; high contrast on the dog's white fur.

**Why it could outperform:** Curiosity: "how do 34,000 soldiers invade an empty island?" Strongest contrast of the four, felt in four words ("invaded. nobody was there."). Emotion: absurd comedy flipping into a sobering, human cost. Universal question: "how do you not notice the enemy left?" Start: confident Allied invasion; end: no victory and hundreds of casualties. Price tag: 34,000 men, 518 shells.

**Evidence:** Wikipedia (Kiska, Battle of the Pips, Operation Cottage), Pacific Wrecks (citing NHHC), NPS page, Task & Purpose, Project 44 (Canadian), dgardner Substack (citing Kostka JFQ), Boomhower blog (Sherrod), ibiblio Hyperwar (Abner Read report), a translated Japanese blog. Limitations: USNI 1958, Kostka JFQ, Garfield, Morison, NHHC narrative and Senshi Sosho NOT opened; the verifier recommends opening Kostka and the NHHC narrative before scripting. Casualty totals conflict even within Wikipedia.

**Originality / saturation:** Medium-low but crowded (H2): ~15 tiny long-form uploads in the last 3 months (mostly under 100 views, several look AI-made; largest 1.4K), Shorts up to 11K; no big channel owns it. The "Pips" half is barely used. Fresh angle: weave the Pips and the evacuation as two halves of one misread, and finish on the human cost. Many creators found it simultaneously, so a good package must stand out from the AI wave.

**Stickman visualisation:** Mascot as a weary soldier in fog: a grey screen with outlines of dots; a radar screen with blinking "pips" (and a cartoon bird); big ships firing at a blank patch of sea; Japanese ships as shadowy silhouettes slipping away; the "invasion" of dogs; fog rendered by pure white/grey backgrounds (cheap, readable).

**5-min fit:** Fits. Cut: Attu detail, Abner Read detail to a sentence, Canadian side, the Funk Jr. human beat (optional), the warning signs debate, the "dogs make coffee" quip.

**Honesty risks:** Do not say "only US soil occupied" (say "the only part of North America Japan occupied"). Do not say the Pips were birds as fact. Keep the Pips (26-27 July) and the evacuation (28 July) separate and do not imply the shells were aimed at the escape fleet. "Eighteen days", not "three weeks". Casualty splits are not documented; do not claim X died of friendly fire vs mines. Abner Read was a floating mine at sea, not a beach casualty. Real deaths: no mocking victims.

**Weakest point:** The casualty numbers, the cause of the Pips and the friendly-fire breakdown are all soft, so the sober ending cannot be given crisp numbers; the story is "darker comedy" with ~300 casualties and real families, which narrows the comedy and risks sounding mocking; the topic is already being mined by AI channels this month.

## D. Captain of Köpenick, 1906

**Proposed title:** He Bought a Used Uniform and Arrested a Mayor
Alternates: (1) The Ex-Convict Who Took Over a Town in a Second-Hand Costume (2) Germany Obeyed a Man in a Costume

**Premise:** A shoemaker who had spent most of his adult life in prison, and was told to leave town because he had no papers, bought a second-hand captain's uniform piecemeal, ordered real soldiers off the street, and took a town hall.

**Opening hook:**
"Berlin, 1906. A man who has spent about a quarter of a century in prison walks up to a squad of Prussian soldiers. He's wearing a used captain's uniform. He says he has orders from above. They follow him. Nobody asks to see anything."

**Central conflict:** A man with no papers against a system that runs on paperwork and uniforms. Stakes: his freedom (a prison record on one side, a town hall on the other); the clock is the 10 days before he is caught. Obedience is the antagonist.

**Escalation steps:**
1. Released in Feb 1906, told to leave the area: no papers, no job; no job, no papers.
2. He buys the uniform piece by piece from second-hand dealers, starting 8 Oct (no price claimed).
3. He tests it: stops a sergeant and grenadiers in the street (16 Oct), then takes more from a shooting range; about ten soldiers in all.
4. Train to Köpenick, guards at the exits.
5. He arrests the mayor and the treasurer for "claimed irregularities" and takes the cash, about 3,500 marks; signs the receipt with the name of the warden of the prison he'd just left (reportedly, de.wikipedia).
6. He changes clothes at a station and vanishes.

**Biggest twist:** Nobody questioned him because of the coat. Second twist: the often-told "passport office" motive: "He later said he wanted a passport. The court didn't believe it, and historians suspect he wanted the money." That is a better beat than the legend.

**Ending / payoff:** Caught 10 days later after a former cellmate told the police; 4 years in December 1906; pardoned by the Kaiser and out after about 20 months (the Kaiser's laugh is "the story goes"). Public sympathy and abroad appeals. Then celebrity: memoir, postcards, tours, a waxwork; Germany coined "Köpenickiade" for a con that works by faking authority. Loop: the man who could not get papers became one of the most famous men in Germany. Epilogue (optional): single-source WWI arrest for wearing a uniform, "reportedly".

**Thumbnail concept:** Focal image: mascot in a too-big captain's uniform with sleeves flapping and a crooked hat, a line of real soldiers snapping to a salute, mascot's eyebrows saying "this is working?" Text: "SALUTE" or "NO PAPERS" (2 words). Mobile read: three-tier hierarchy (tiny mascot in a big coat, rank of soldiers saluting) reads instantly; the comic mismatch of scale is the hook. Colours: Prussian blue and grey, red details, scarf the only yellow (maybe worn over the uniform).

**Why it could outperform:** Curiosity: "how do you fool real soldiers with a costume?" (universal: could I?). Emotion: delight at a nobody beating the system, then a sweet ending (celebrity). Best fit for the mascot's "worst deal in history, gets the break" voice. Start (no papers, no job) -> end (famous, pardoned). Price tag: about 3,500 marks, plus 25 years in prison behind him and a 10-day clock.

**Evidence:** Strong: de.wikipedia (cites Löschburg, Heidelmeyer, Voigt's memoir, verdict reprint), Deutsche Biographie, LTO, Irish Times, Berlin district pages, HistoryNet, en.wikipedia lead. Limitations: Löschburg, Heidelmeyer, the 1909 memoir, the 1906 verdict and contemporary press NOT opened; the receipt detail rests on de.wikipedia plus a snippet.

**Originality / saturation:** Medium in English. H3: one 82K video 4 months old, Plainly Difficult 28K (9 years old), House of History 6.1K; the 1956 film has 748K views; about 20 small videos/Shorts; German-language competition strong. Fresh angle: the "he said he wanted a passport; the court didn't believe it" frame, and the story of obedience rather than the usual "bumbling thief" telling. A gentler, funnier tone than most competitors.

**Stickman visualisation:** Mascot as Voigt: scarf, oversized coat; cheap props (hat, medals, buttons); soldiers as identical faceless stick figures; a mayor with a tiny crown of paper; a receipt with a name stamped; a waxwork of himself; the word "Köpenickiade" as a dictionary page. Easy, funny, and visually varied.

**5-min fit:** Fits comfortably. Cut: the exact count of soldiers, phone lines (single source), the Berlin neutral-ground details, mayor's alleged irregularities, funeral salutes, WWI arrest unless confirmed, the sewage fund detail.

**Honesty risks:** Passport motive: attribute and hedge. Kaiser: "the story goes that he laughed"; the pardon is fact, linked to public pressure. Not "4,000 marks" (about 3,500; the receipt said 4,000.70 because of a clerk's miscount). Not "cost 18 marks". "About ten soldiers". Mayor not proven corrupt: "claimed irregularities". The heist was planned over weeks, so do not frame it as a spur-of-the-moment accident. No "full military honours" funeral line.

**Weakest point:** The popular emotional core, "a poor man's desperate quest for a passport", is mostly legend shaped by the play, and the honest version (he probably wanted money, planned for weeks) makes him less lovable; the ending (celebrity) is warm but diffuse; an 82K English competitor is only four months old, and German-language rivals are strong.

## Comparison (observations only, no scoring or predictions)

| | A Reis | B Humbert | C Kiska | D Köpenick |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Contrast in ~4 words | "real notes, fake authority" | "locked safe, 20 years" | "invaded, nobody there" | "costume, real soldiers" |
| One thumbnail image | two identical notes | sealed safe | soldier vs tiny dog | tiny captain, saluting soldiers |
| English saturation | moderate | low (unproven demand) | crowded, tiny AI channels | moderate, one recent 82K |
| Fact softness | moderate | highest (contents, totals) | high (casualties, Pips) | moderate (passport, Kaiser) |
| Tone fit for lazy sarcastic mascot | good | good | risky (real deaths) | best |
| Ending flips/loops | bank pays, printer pays | the safe holds almost nothing | invasion captures nothing | outcast becomes celebrity |

Self-critical note: C has the strongest four-word hook but the heaviest tone risk and softest numbers; D has the best character fit but a legend problem at its centre; A has the strongest mechanism but the weakest mid-section visuals and the most competition; B has the cleanest object but a payoff we can only hedge. The recommended path is to take two of these to Stage 2 with their weakest points explicitly tested.
