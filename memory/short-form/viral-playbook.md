# Viral Playbook: how Shorts win (rewritten 2026-11-01)
The fundamentals behind every idea, title, first frame, thumbnail, story and script. Read it as judgment, not a checklist.
`learnings.md` holds what OUR data has validated and the hypotheses we are testing; when they disagree with this file, our data wins.

**The goal:** videos people choose to watch, keep watching, finish, and feel were worth it (sometimes watch again, share or comment).
**The standard:** a proven concept with a fresh angle, accurate, built into a story with a real payoff, told in memorable stickman pictures.

---

## 1. How a Short gets watched (creator's view, not the algorithm's secret rules)
- **Most Shorts views come from the Shorts feed, where the video autoplays.** There the viewer never sees the thumbnail: the **first frame + first line** are the packaging, and the decision is "swipe or stay" in about a second. Thumbnail and title matter on the channel page, search, Home shelves and subscriptions. (Widely reported by creators; confirm with our own traffic sources in Studio.)
- YouTube shows a Short to more people when the people it was shown to stay, finish, rewatch and engage, and don't swipe away. We can't see the exact rules and never pretend to.
- What each number tells us:
  | Metric (Studio) | Mostly tests |
  |---|---|
  | Viewed vs swiped away | the idea + first frame + first line |
  | Retention graph / avg % viewed | the story (where it sags is where a beat failed) |
  | Rewatch spikes, loop % over 100 | the ending, visual density |
  | Comments, shares, subs | the payoff and whether it made people feel/think something |
  | CTR (channel/search/Home only) | title + thumbnail |
- Never trade satisfaction for retention: a hook the video doesn't deliver loses trust and the next video's audience.

## 2. The idea comes first
A strong script cannot rescue a weak idea. **One-sentence test:** if someone heard the idea in one sentence, would they immediately want to know what happens? If not, improve it or reject it. Reject ideas that only sound good after an explanation.
Strong ideas usually have one or more of:
- a question people already wonder about (OminiAM: "What if every human disappeared?" 1M vs ~10K typical);
- a situation you can imagine yourself in (Ink Story "Your life as every rank in the U.S. Army in WW2" 438K vs ~84K);
- a contradiction or surprising consequence;
- two people or interests in conflict;
- a familiar situation taken to an extreme;
- a mystery whose answer is short but satisfying.
Evidence from our competitors: **premise beats format.** OminiAM's identical format got 1M on "every human disappeared" and 3-6K on "oil disappears" / "America not discovered". Ink Story's rank series hit on the U.S. and German armies and stayed low on the Soviet and Japanese ones. **For history: choose it because something compelling happens to someone** (they have something to lose, something unexpected happens, there is a reveal or consequence), from any era or continent, never because the facts are interesting or the region is familiar.
**Copies die:** the "Why clothes?" thumbnail on 6 channels: 4.8M for the first, 21K / 5.6K / 1.8K for the copies. Keep the appeal, change the story.

## 3. The hook opens a question the video answers
- The first frame shows the situation; the first line opens the question. No intros, no context dump, no "did you know".
- A shocking sentence is not a hook unless it leads into the story. Promise only what the video delivers.
- Good shapes: a situation you're in ("Everyone's gone. It's just you and one stranger."), a contradiction ("Think pirates fought all day? Not even close."), a stakes question, a strange image that begs "why?".

## 4. Story beats information
We make entertaining stories, not lectures. Before researching details, find:
- **who** the story is about (you, a person, a group; a stickman needs someone to follow),
- **what they want or face**, **what makes it hard**, **what changes**,
- **what the viewer is waiting to find out**, and **the reveal / consequence / twist** at the end.
Show information through events, choices and pictures. Background that fits in one sentence gets one sentence. For history, science or psychology: find the human situation first; facts support it.
**One fascinating story with a few well-chosen facts beats ten facts joined by narration.** Use a fact only if it improves understanding, tension, credibility or the payoff. Numbers, jargon and caveats cost attention; spend them only where they pay. Accuracy is never optional: we choose facts, we never bend them. Caveats the story doesn't need go in the description.

## 5. Every beat must earn its place
Each beat introduces something new, raises the stakes, changes the situation, or lands a feeling, and moves toward the payoff. The viewer should feel the situation getting more interesting.
Cut: repeating a point in new words, facts kept "because they're interesting", stretches with no development, jokes that stop the story, fake cliffhangers, padding to hit a length. Don't manufacture a twist every few seconds; developments must follow from each other.

## 6. Emotion
Facts inform; feelings make it memorable. Pick the journey the subject offers (curiosity, anticipation, surprise, tension, humor, empathy, injustice, hope, relief, a satisfying realization) and decide what the viewer should feel at the start, middle and end. Don't force the same arc every time. Humor comes from the situation and Mr. Yellow Scarf's honest reactions; it never replaces the story and never mocks real suffering.

## 7. Visual storytelling (we are a stickman channel)
- Write what the viewer SEES while writing what they hear. If a picture can explain it, the narration doesn't have to.
- Concrete, simple, memorable: expressions, gestures, interactions, props, contrasts, simple diagrams, a changing environment. Each beat a different image; never the same gag twice (empty sack, empty jar, empty bowl = one idea, three times).
- The first frame is designed first: one clear situation, readable on a phone in a second, ideally with a face reacting.

## 8. Payoff
The ending answers the question or fulfills the promise of the hook, ideally in a way that changes how the viewer sees what came before: a surprising explanation, an unexpected consequence, a reversal, a realization. Not a summary, not a forced moral, not a pun stapled on. If we can't say in one sentence why the ending satisfies, the story isn't ready.

## 9. Packaging: title, first frame, thumbnail
- Designed at the IDEA stage, not after production. If we can't package an idea, it isn't a Shorts idea.
- Title: the question or claim, ≤ ~60 chars, keywords first (SEO format in learnings). Title and thumbnail tell one story together without repeating each other.
- Thumbnail (channel page, search, Home): one focal emotion, a contrast character, 2-3 words that add to the title, text between 12% and 88% of the height.
- **Female co-star:** use her when she is part of the premise (the other person in a hypothetical, a relationship, the source of a conflict or mystery, a woman's own story), and put her in the first frame, not only the thumbnail. Our evidence so far: in every competitor hit with a woman we recorded, she was part of the premise, never decoration (Relic "Why clothes?" 4.8M, Lost Legacy "Your Life as a Concubine" 4.46M, Awaken "Can 2 People Restart Humanity?" 3.2M), and copies using the same woman still flopped. Roughly 1 video in 3 when the story fits; never forced. Stylish, confident, expressive, fully clothed, never suggestive. Results tracked in the video log (`co_star`).

## 10. Originality and research
- Borrow the mechanism (why the source works), never the script, jokes, characters or art. Our version needs a different question, experience or payoff.
- Research proportional to the story: verify the premise and every claim the narration makes; most research never reaches the script. No invented dialogue, quotes, numbers, motives or events. Real suffering gets its context.

## 11. Engagement (earned, never faked)
End with something people want to answer or argue about only when it fits; pin it. Reply early. Series and related videos keep people watching. Never: bots, bought views, sub4sub, fake comments, "like if..." bait.

## 12. Learning
Separate what the data shows, what we infer, and what we test next. One video proves nothing; look for patterns across videos and say what else differed (topic, title, length, time). Change one thing per batch so results can be compared.

## 13. Retention craft: shape, loops, lines (Joe adopted 2026-11-12)
Source: Higgsfield's faceless-video manuals + skills review (research/short-form/2026-11-12-higgsfield-skills-review.md). Applies to Shorts AND long-form. These are craft rules, not channel data; performance-review tests them like everything else.
- **The failure mode is a list.** Facts are material; shape is the product.
- **Hook:** the first sentence is ≤ 8 words, the most surprising concrete thing stated flat (understatement makes a big number bigger). No windup clause, no greeting. A question hook is fine if the answer waits for the payoff.
- **Early stakes:** one beat that answers "why should I care" (a misconception, or it touches the viewer).
- **Through-line object:** one physical object or process that appears in every beat and gets steadily worse/bigger (a fuse burning down, a debt pile growing until it blocks the door, a map filling with dots). The payoff resolves it. If the ending can't pay it off, pick another object.
- **Causal links:** every beat leads to the next with "because / therefore / but / unless". If a link only works as "and another fact", connect it, move it or cut it.
- **Reorder test:** if the middle beats could be shuffled without loss, it's a list. Each beat must be bigger, weirder or more specific than the last.
- **Turn test:** the twist must change what the viewer believed two beats ago. A summary is not a turn.
- **Kicker:** the last line echoes the hook's image or number with a new meaning; it doesn't repeat it.
- **Open loops:** the main question stays open until the payoff. Open the next small question BEFORE closing the current one, so there's never a moment when everything is answered. Loops must be specific (a prediction, a mystery object, a countdown, "the strangest part is how it ended"), never vague ("wait till you see this"). Max 2-3 open at once, every one paid off better than expected (no fake cliffhangers). **Shorts:** main question at frame 0 + one re-hook at ~20-25 s (where most people swipe) + the answer, then the loop cut. **Long-form:** a re-hook at least every 45-60 s; every chapter ends on a hand-over hook ("that plan was about to cost him everything").
- **Line hygiene:** every line adds ONE new concrete thing (number, place, object, comparison). No filler ("basically", "you know", "so yeah", "let's talk about"), one modifier per thing, no content word repeated within ~6 words, no 5+ word phrase used twice. Too short? Add a fact, never an adjective. Too long? Cut modifiers first, facts last. No chains of rhetorical questions that never get answered.
- **Show what the line names:** if the line says spices, spices are on screen. A picture that could fit any line gets redrawn.
- **What-if premise contract:** "We change X, keep Y fixed, follow Z, answer Q." One stipulated impossible event, then consistent consequences: no extra miracles. Settle ambiguous premises first (size vs mass, sudden vs gradual, temporary vs permanent); often that choice IS the story.
