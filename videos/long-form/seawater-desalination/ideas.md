## 1. He Crossed the Atlantic in a Rubber Boat With No Water. On Purpose.
Pillar 4 (unbelievable real story). Alt titles: "The Doctor Who Tried to Live on the Ocean" / "He Didn't Bring Water Across the Atlantic"

- **One-line premise:** In 1952 a French doctor, Alain Bombard, set out alone from the Canary Islands in a small rubber dinghy to prove that shipwrecked people do not have to die of thirst by rule, and he drifted about 4,400 km to Barbados in 65 days.
- **Opening hook (first line):** "1952. A doctor leaves the Canary Islands alone in a rubber boat. He takes no water. On purpose."
- **Who the story follows:** the real man (named, since it is history) as a lone, stubborn, slightly lazy-looking everyman. The mascot can stand in as the boat's only passenger if Joe wants the brand voice; not a dramatised fake identity.
- **Conflict and stakes:** one man against a rule every sailor was trained to follow ("never drink seawater"), with his own life as the test subject. Clock: food, weather, the body wearing down.
- **Escalation:** fish and plankton as food; small amounts of seawater; rain that came most days (per a critic); weight loss of about 25 kg; day 53 a British freighter (Arakaka) meets him, its crew say he is still over 1,000 km short, and he declines passage. The freighter's meal gave him the worst stomach trouble of the voyage. Landing in Barbados on 23 December, then brief hospital.
- **Twist:** he never claimed seawater alone keeps you alive. His own test was less clean than his slogan (rain, a freighter stop). The rule he attacked was partly right, and so was he.
- **Payoff:** the honest version, not a miracle. Closing line idea (narration): "He proved the rule was wrong. Or half wrong. The sea is still the judge." Legacy line from the source: "still debated."
- **Why a stranger clicks:** a one-sentence contradiction (no water, on purpose) plus a survival clock and a man who will not quit.
- **Visual potential (cartoon):** a tiny dinghy on a huge blue sea (strong); harpoon and fish; a sail; a freighter passing; belt-hole weight-loss gag; Barbados beach as the last frame. Simple repeatable props.
- **Facts and sources:**
  - Departure Las Palmas 19 Oct 1952; landing Stroude Bay, Barbados, 23 Dec 1952; about 4,400 km; about 65 days. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alain_Bombard (fetched). Amusing Planet agrees on the 65 days (snippet): https://www.amusingplanet.com/2025/12/alain-bombard-biologist-who-shipwrecked.html
  - Food: fish (harpoon and hooks) and plankton (small net). Fish juice supplied fresh-ish liquid. Wikipedia (fetched).
  - Freighter Arakaka on day 53; he declined passage; meal caused stomach trouble. Wikipedia (fetched).
  - Weight loss about 25 kg; briefly hospitalised in Barbados. Wikipedia (fetched).
  - Books: Naufragé Volontaire (1953), The Voyage of the Heretique (1953). Wikipedia (fetched).
  - Caribbean Beat (snippet): https://www.caribbean-beat.com/issue-172/alain-bombard-the-voluntary-castaway-on-this-day
- **What is disputed or exaggerated:**
  - Amount of seawater: Wikipedia says "limited amounts"; a snippet from Amusing Planet says about 0.75 L a day mixed with fish juice. Use "small amounts" unless a second opened source gives a number.
  - Rain: Hannes Lindemann (critic) said he needed rain water "most days" (Wikipedia). The voyage account does not mention rain (Wikipedia).
  - Navy claim: Wikipedia says French and Taiwanese navies "concurred"; the Taiwanese "134 days" is unclear. Do not use the navy line as proof.
  - Film: "The Voyage of the Heretique" (2017, Didier Nion) is a 93-minute fiction, not evidence.
  - Bombard's own response to the freighter criticism: NOT verified.
- **Saturation:** searches returned no YouTube results for this story; could not tell how big existing videos are. Check YouTube directly before scripting.
- **5-minute fit:** good. Beats: cold open (0:00-0:30), departure and the rule (0:30-1:15), days 1-20 thirst and fish (1:15-2:30), day 53 freighter (2:30-3:45), landing and legacy (3:45-5:00).
- **Risks:** medical sensitivity. The story must never encourage drinking seawater; narration must say what happened, not what to do. Joe's rule: no spoken disclaimers, so honesty goes in the narration ("by his own account", "critics say"). Part of the story is legend; hedge.

## 2. On the Caspian Sea, a Nuclear Reactor Ran the City's Taps
Pillar 4 (unbelievable real story). Alt titles: "The Reactor That Made a Soviet City's Drinking Water" / "The Nuclear Plant That Gave a City Water"

- **One-line premise:** In Soviet Kazakhstan a sodium-cooled fast reactor at Aktau (then Shevchenko) on the Caspian Sea produced power, heat and roughly 120,000 m3 of drinking water a day from seawater, until the state could no longer pay for its fuel.
- **Opening hook (first line):** "In 1973 the Soviet Union switched on a nuclear reactor by the sea, and its first job was making the town's drinking water."
- **Who the story follows:** the reactor as the main character (cartoon reactor, a squat drum with straw-like pipes into the Caspian), with the town's engineers and families as the people it serves. Mascot as a worker on a shift.
- **Conflict and stakes:** a whole city's water depends on one machine that needs fuel money. Stakes: water and power for a coastal city, and the cost of shutting it down.
- **Escalation:** construction 1964; reaches criticality late 1972; commercial start 16 July 1973; the plant also made plutonium; the project's official lifetime ended 1993; June 1994 forced shutdown because the money to buy fuel ran out; ran far below capacity; operations ceased 1999.
- **Twist:** the water was a side job. The main purpose included plutonium production (per the sources), so the city's tap was attached to a weapons-material plant. Verify wording before using "weapons": say "plutonium production" and cite.
- **Payoff:** the bill. Decommissioning was estimated at about $330 million (2020 estimate, to be paid through the electricity tariff, per Wikipedia). The city pays for the machine that gave it water.
- **Why a stranger clicks:** nuclear plus sea plus drinking water is a one-sentence contradiction; most people do not know reactors have made water.
- **Visual potential (cartoon):** very strong. Reactor drum, glowing sodium coolant (soft orange), Caspian waves, a tap with a nuclear hazard sign, town skyline, a tiny worker with a yellow scarf at the control room door.
- **Facts and sources:**
  - Construction began 1964; first electricity 1973; desalination about 120,000 m3 of fresh water a day for Aktau; sodium in primary and secondary circuits; forced shutdown June 1994 (fuel funds); decommission date 22 April 1999; $330M 2020 estimate. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BN-350_reactor (fetched).
  - Operating dates, output, and "first industrial-scale seawater desalination reactor" (NTI, snippet). Source: https://world-nuclear.org/nuclear-reactor-database/details/Aktau (database snippet) and the NTI line quoted in search results.
  - Kazakh press (Russian language, snippet): https://tengrinews.kz/article/kazahstane-byila-aes-proizoshlo-reaktorom-bn-350-2490/
- **What is disputed or exaggerated:**
  - Water output: Wikipedia 120,000 m3/day; other sources 100,000 to 120,000 m3/day; one reference gives "up to 100,000 tonnes a day". Use "about 100,000 to 120,000 cubic metres".
  - Electrical output: 350 MWe (Wikipedia) vs 52 MWe net / 90 MWe gross (World Nuclear Association database) vs 135 MWe (another). Avoid a MWe number on screen.
  - "First" claim (NTI): one source; hedge.
  - Post-1999 water supply for Aktau: NOT verified. This is a research gap that decides the ending.
- **Saturation:** no YouTube video surfaced in search; the topic is niche in English but the nuclear audience is large. Could not tell.
- **5-minute fit:** good if the post-1999 water question is answered. Beats: cold open, the 1973 switch-on, the two jobs, the 1994 fuel cut, the 1999 switch-off, the decommissioning bill.
- **Risks:** nuclear fear framing; keep it factual and non-graphic. Story is thinner than premise 1 (funding decline is a slow arc). Needs one human character to carry it.

## 3. Kuwait Bought Its Drinking Water by Boat
Pillar 2 (origins of a familiar thing). Alt titles: "The Gulf Port That Imported Its Water by Boat" / "Before the Sea Made Water, Boats Brought It"

- **One-line premise:** Before desalination, a city on the Gulf got its drinking water by dhow from the Shatt al-Arab river in Iraq, until the state bought up the wells and built plants that make water from the same sea.
- **Opening hook (first line):** "Kuwait sits on the Gulf. For decades, its drinking water came by boat from another country."
- **Who the story follows:** a water carrier with a pole (kandar) and tanks, or a dhow crew; mascot as a porter balancing two cans on a pole. A real named person is needed for the story to work (none found yet).
- **Conflict and stakes:** rain fell away after 1907 and the wells could not keep up. Stakes: whether the city can drink at all. Clock: the next boat, the next storm.
- **Escalation:** informal dhow runs; a company formed in 1939 to run water carriers; the state buys all private wells by 1951; the first desalination plant opens 1953 (or 1951 by another account).
- **Twist:** the sea that surrounded the port was the answer all along; the boats were a workaround for a problem the sea could solve.
- **Payoff:** the water boats end. Closing image: the same blue sea, now producing taps. Possible extra beat (single source, UNVERIFIED): the first plant was built by an oil company for its own staff.
- **Why a stranger clicks:** "a coastal city bought its water by boat" is a surprise fact in one line. Universal question: how does a city survive without rain?
- **Visual potential (cartoon):** good. Dhows loaded with tanks, donkeys with qirab (leather containers), porters on a market street, rows of wells, a plant on the shore.
- **Facts and sources:**
  - Kuwait had no rivers; relied on rain and wells; after rainfall fell sharply in 1907, residents began importing water from the Shatt al-Arab. Source: https://kuwaittimes.com/article/30803/lifestyle/how-kuwait-quenched-its-thirst/ (fetched).
  - "49 large dhows" carrying "around 80,000 gallons" a day (hedged, fetched). Water carried by porters, donkeys/camels, kandar carriers (fetched).
  - Water transport company set up 1939; government bought all private wells by 1951; first desalination plant 1953, capacity 1 million gallons a day (fetched). The same article also says the well purchase "coincided" with the first plant in 1951, which conflicts.
  - Snippets only (open before use): Hindelang 2021 academic PDF https://boris.unibe.ch/164433/1/Hindelang_2021_-_Precious_Property.pdf ; a British 1910 report of 40-50 tanked boats (figure from a secondary summary); fleet of 45 by 1946; Kuwait Oil Company's small plant at Mina Al-Ahmadi in 1951.
- **What is disputed or exaggerated:**
  - Start dates: 1907 (fetched) vs 1909 vs 1925 (snippets).
  - Boat counts and volumes: fetched 49 dhows / 80,000 gallons (hedged) vs snippet 40-50 boats of 3,000-5,000 gallons each.
  - Basra: not in the fetched article (snippet only).
  - Storm-and-famine claim (a three-day storm could cause a water famine): snippet only, UNVERIFIED.
  - First plant: 1951 (KOC) vs 1953 (Shuwaikh).
- **Saturation:** no YouTube video surfaced; English-language coverage looks thin. Could not tell.
- **5-minute fit:** fits, but needs a human thread. Beats: cold open (the boats), the drought, the trade, the storm risk, the wells bought, the plant, the boats gone.
- **Risks:** this is an interesting origin but the stakes are soft without a person. Needs a second fetched source before any script. Low on drama without a named character.

## 4. The Water Company Owed 34,000 Investors S$900 Million
Pillar 5 (business, money, power). Alt titles: "Singapore's Water Company Bet on Electricity and Lost" / "The Water Plant That Was Supposed to Pay for Itself"

- **One-line premise:** A Singapore water company built a plant that made drinking water and sold electricity to cover the cost of the water; when power prices fell and banks would not lend, it sold preference shares to retail investors, collapsed in 2018 and now faces a trial of its former leaders.
- **Opening hook (first line):** "The plant made drinking water from the sea. It was supposed to pay for itself by selling electricity."
- **Who the story follows:** founder Olivia Lum (accused, not convicted) and the roughly 34,000 small investors who held the company's perpetual and preference securities.
- **Conflict and stakes:** a bet on the electricity market against banks that said no. Stakes: S$900 million owed to investors (press figure, "roughly"). Clock: the company's debt and the 2018 court protection.
- **Escalation (per reports, mostly allegations for the trial parts):**
  - The Tuaspring project was built so power sales would offset desalination's energy cost.

## 5. What If the Sea Stopped Giving You Water for a Week?
Pillar 3 (what-if, real anchor). Alt titles: "What If Every Desalination Plant Stopped for One Week?" / "The Sea Stops Making Your Water. Day 1."

- **One-line premise:** If the desalination plants that supply a coastal city went offline together for a week, the first things to go would be the lawns, the farms and the car washes; the real question is how long the city's stored water and its boats last.
- **Opening hook (first line):** "In late August 2026, most of Israel's big desalination plants went offline in the same week. Officials said homes would keep their water. The farms did not."
- **Who the story follows:** a hypothetical coastal city and its residents, with the mascot as the everyman in a queue for water, then as the tanker-truck driver.
- **Conflict and stakes:** each day a service fails (garden, car wash, restaurants, farms, the tap). Stakes: how many days of water the city really has.
- **Escalation:** Day 1 gardens and cars; Day 2 rationing and queues; Day 3 reserves run down (the figures conflict, see below); Day 5 water trucks, the same answer the 1900s city used.
- **Twist:** the city survives; the first casualty is the garden and the vegetable farmer, and the second is the tanker trucks. Callback to premise 3: the Gulf's old answer to a dry sea was a boat.
- **Payoff:** a funny but real closing line about gardens and lawns: "The lawn died first. You were fine. Mostly." Keep hypotheticals labelled as imagination.
- **Why a stranger clicks:** a real event opens the video; "What if" escalates to a universal question about your own tap.
- **Visual potential (cartoon):** good. Desal plant on a shore; empty taps; a garden hose; a water truck; a queue of stick figures; sprinkler lawn going brown. Real-event title card avoided (no on-screen text rule).
- **Facts and sources:**
  - Israel late August to early September 2026: five of six big Mediterranean desalination plants offline after seawater turbidity rose; Hadera kept running. Smart Water Magazine (snippet): https://smartwatermagazine.com/news/smart-water-magazine/five-of-israels-six-mediterranean-desalination-plants-offline-as-seawater-turbidity-halts-filtration-developing
