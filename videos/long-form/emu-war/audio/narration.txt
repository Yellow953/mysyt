In 1932, the Australian army went to war. The enemy was emus. Four November, 1932. The machine gun jams. And about a thousand emus keep walking toward the water. And somebody had to pay for the bullets. Say there was a gunner. Let's call him Mr. Yellow Scarf. He's a soldier-settler. He survived the trenches, then got a block of marginal wheat land in Western Australia. So far, his verdict: "I survived the Kaiser for this." The Depression had wheat prices in a headlock. Prices kept falling, and by most accounts, farmers threatened to hold back their harvest. Then, by most accounts, drought pushed the emus into the wheat country, looking for water. One estimate puts it at about twenty thousand emus. So a deputation of farmers, many of them ex-soldiers, went to Defence and asked for machine guns. Defence Minister George Pearce said yes. On conditions. Western Australia pays for transport. The farmers pay for the bullets, the bunks and the bread. Worst deal in history. Our gunner said yes anyway, because the wheat wasn't going to sell itself. On 2 November, the operation began at Campion. Major G.P.W. Meredith, two Lewis guns, and 10,000 rounds of ammunition. Day one: about fifty emus spotted, perhaps a dozen dead. Our gunner calls that a good start. On 4 November, at a dam, the gun jammed. That's the jam from the start. One line from the time says emus can face machine guns "with the invulnerability of tanks." Then one politician told parliament the emus deserved medals for winning every round so far. Around 8 November, the government pulled the army out. Then the farmers pushed back. Around 13 November, the guns went back out. Meredith's report claims 986 birds dead from 9,860 rounds. The campaign ended 10 December. Meredith was recalled. The army went home. The birds didn't. So what finally worked? First, the law. The Game Act of 1874 made killing emus from June to September an offence. Wrong answer. Then, in 1922, the story flipped. Farmers in the Upper Chapman district complained. On 6 August, emus were listed as vermin. By mid-1923, many districts wanted the same listing. So the government paid a bounty on each emu, tallied shire by shire. The proof was the beak. Years on, our gunner is still farming. He files his own claim slip. Beak, tally, paid. In the mid-1930s, one count says 57,034 emus were destroyed in a single Northampton season. If that holds, that's about 300 a day. Meredith's count managed about 26. In July 1943, the Chief Guardian of Game put it in writing. Emus have been declared vermin in so many districts that we who administer the conservation law, the Game Act, have virtually lost control. Say our gunner's paddock is one of those dots. It's the only land he has. In 1943, the government added free ammunition for local vermin boards. It also ran hunts by motor vehicle. Not the army. Farmers, by most accounts, had asked for it back more than once. Each request, accounts say, was refused. From 1945 to 1960, the rate was one shilling for each beak. In those years, bounties went out on 284,724 beaks. In 1947, protection came back in one south-west corner. Then the system got gamed. Birds shot outside the fence were carried inside and claimed. Later government tallies, up to 1973, say the figures "cannot be relied upon." As far as the records go, the gun never came back. Our gunner, at the counter with the last slip, watches the emus walk past the fence. He doesn't reach for the gun. He reaches for the pencil, and the emus keep walking. I survived the Kaiser and a war with birds. The least you can do is subscribe.
