## 2. SPECIAL FOCUS 1: scene 24 causal claim
Script line: "That question is why rent became the biggest piece of what franchisees pay."

What I found
- For the structure: Scribner says Sonneborn "created a leaseback system that quickly generated the money Kroc needed to expand"; Wikipedia says the "Sonneborn model" persists; Motley Fool (Brownlee) says Franchise Realty "was launched in 1956"; HowStuffWorks (via Wikipedia, not opened) describes lease cost passed on with a markup. So there is good support that the landlord idea created the rent-on-sales relationship with franchisees.
- For "rent is the biggest piece today": 10-K figures show rents ahead of royalties in every year I saw: 2021 8,381 vs 4,645; 2022 9,046 vs 5,006; 2023 9,840 vs 5,531; 2024 10,017 vs 5,606; 2025 10,442 vs 6,018 (stock-analysis-on.net; matches research.md). Search summaries of earlier 10-Ks: 2014 6,107 vs 3,085; 2017 6,496 vs 3,519; 2020 6,845 vs 3,832. Ratio drifted from about 2.0x (2014) to about 1.7x (2025).
- WHEN did rent first exceed royalties: NOT documented in anything I could open. Pre-2010s filings I opened (FY2004) lump "continuing rent and service fees" and show no split in the parts I read; the earliest split I have is 2011 (rent 5,718.5). I found no business-history source stating the crossover year. Claims in blogs/tweets (20-40% markup, "5% of sales whichever greater", "rent 64% of revenue") are secondary and unverified.
- Later developments clearly matter: the 10-K says minimum rent is "based on the Company's underlying investment in owned sites" and "parallels the Company's underlying lease obligations", and the company now owns about 56% of land and 80% of buildings (consolidated markets), with a $14.6B lease ROU asset. So the size of today's rent line reflects decades of site purchases, lease growth and percent-of-sales rent on ever-higher sales, not just a 1950s idea. I found no historian (Love, Boas & Chain, case study) text I could open that traces rent's share causally.
- "Biggest piece of what franchisees pay": the 10-K only supports "biggest piece of what franchisees pay McDonald's's franchise revenue" (vs royalties and fees). Franchisees also pay advertising contributions, food, labor, etc. (Motley Fool quoting WSJ: royalty 5%, advertising 5%, rent up to 16% of sales; secondary).

Verdict: the causal chain "Sonneborn's idea -> rent exists as a sales-linked charge" is SUPPORTED; the stronger chain "-> and that is why rent is the largest component today" is UNVERIFIED (plausible, undocumented, and confounded by later land purchases). Do not state it as fact.

Most defensible replacement (two options):
- A: "That question put the company in the landlord's chair. And today, rent is the biggest piece of what franchisees pay it: about ten point four billion in 2025, against about six billion in royalties."
- B (more conservative): "That question is why McDonald's charges rent at all. In 2025 that rent came to about ten point four billion, against about six billion in royalties."
Option B is fully defensible; option A is defensible if "pay it" (McDonald's) is kept and "biggest" is limited to rent versus royalties/fees. Avoid "is why rent became the biggest."

## 3. SPECIAL FOCUS 2: finance (scenes 15-21, 24-26)
(a) Is the 10-K figure gross rent revenue? YES, as far as I read. The rent figure is a component of "Revenues from franchised restaurants" ($16,548M), a revenue line. The costs sit elsewhere: income statement line "Franchised restaurants-occupancy expenses" $2,618M (2025), $2,536M (2024), $2,475M (2023), and the MD&A definition: "Franchised restaurant margins are measured as revenues from franchised restaurants less franchised restaurant occupancy costs", with occupancy costs including "lease expense and depreciation". So $10.4B is before the company's lease expense and depreciation on those sites, and before corporate overhead. (Direct arithmetic: 16,548 - 2,618 = 13,930, which matches the margin figure in research.md.) I did not read the Leasing note, so I cannot state the lessee lease-cost or sublease-income figures.
(b) "The company pays its own landlord": accurate only for LEASED sites. The 10-K says it "generally owns or secures a long-term lease", owns about 56% of land and 80% of buildings in consolidated markets, and carries lease liabilities of $14.8B (694 + 14,147), so many sites are leased (and those leases ARE paid to third-party landlords). For owned land there is no landlord; the cost is capital investment, property tax and depreciation, and minimum rent is "based on the Company's underlying investment in owned sites". Recommended scene 16: "Say the company rents the site itself, and pays its own landlord sixty thousand." (or keep the line since it sits under the DRAMATIZATION overlay, but one qualifier is cheap).
(c) Does $10.4B rent vs $6.0B royalties say anything about profit? NO. They are revenues. Rent carries the company's occupancy costs (lease expense, depreciation: $2.6B in total for franchised sites, my reading of the income statement); royalties carry no comparable direct site cost, so a royalty dollar could be MORE profitable than a rent dollar. The 10-K reports franchised margin (13,930) in aggregate, not by rent vs royalty (I read no such split). Confusion risk: MODERATE. The title word "Fortune" plus "landlord" invites "McDonald's makes most of its profit from rent" (research.md #7: do not claim). Scenes 24-25 do the right thing. To close the gap add the cost clause: scene 25 "The filing doesn't say which earns more profit. Rent is revenue, before the company pays its own costs for those sites. It only shows which brings in more." Keep the on-screen "revenue" tag in scene 24.

## 4. SPECIAL FOCUS 3 and the Sonneborn quote
- Scene 12 (rents a plot, re-rents): see row 12. Today the structure is "lease and license" (10-K search quote) and the company "owns or secures a long-term lease". Historic markup claims (20% rising to 40%; "or 5% of sales whichever greater") come from HowStuffWorks (via Wikipedia) and Motley Fool (quoting "as much as a 40% markup ... or 5% of sales, whichever was greater"), both secondary; the script correctly states no markup.
- Scene 20 leverage: no 10-K sentence says "site control gives leverage". Evidence is structural: the franchise arrangement includes a lease and a license; the company controls long-term occupancy ("long-term leases ensure long-term occupancy rights and help to control related costs", Motley Fool quoting 10-K language, and FY2004 10-K Item 2). A 2014-era academic contract analysis summary (search result; PDF unreadable to me) says McDonald's termination clause covers non-compliance with any agreement including a lease; not confirmed. A Trung Phan tweet says breaking rules risks eviction: weak, unsourced. So "has leverage" is SUPPORTED as logical inference only; do not claim incidents. The 1995 McLibel transcript shows the company's own witness saying it cannot simply say "you are out", so avoid implying easy eviction.
- Scene 21 royalty-plus-rent: VERIFIED for conventional franchisees; not for developmental licensees (royalty only).
- Scene 7: SUPPORTED simplification (row 7).
- Sonneborn quote ("We are not technically in the food business. We are in the real estate business. The only reason we sell [fifteen-cent] hamburgers is because they are the greatest producer of revenue, from which our tenants can pay us our rent."): first print appearance NOT FOUND. Only lead: footnotebrief.com says it is "recorded in John F. Love's 1995 book McDonald's: Behind the Arches" (secondary; I could not open the book or confirm a page). Wikipedia's Sonneborn page does not carry it; Goodreads/Find a Grave/blogs repeat it with no citation; the "fifteen-cent" clause appears only in some versions. The script's wording ("widely attributed ... whether he said it, I couldn't find") is honest; optionally add "it may trace to a 1995 book on McDonald's" only if Love's text is checked first.
