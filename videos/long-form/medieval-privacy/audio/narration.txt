You've waited months for tonight. Your wife is right beside you. So is your mother. Your father. Three little brothers. And a grandmother who has never once slept. One room. Eight people. And you need five minutes alone. You have a plan. It's a bad plan. But it's a plan. Plan A. The village walks you to bed. In some places, that's tradition: candles, songs, advice you did not ask for. The last guest winks at you. You have never wanted to hurt a stranger so much. They finally go. Your wife looks at you. You look at her. Your mother clears her throat from the bench. Plan A is dead. Plan B. "Let's get some air." Your wife takes your hand and you slip out into the dark field. It's quiet. It's cold. It's perfect. Then a lantern bobs up from the hedge. Then another. Then six. Turns out you were not the first newlyweds to ask for some air. Somebody's cousin waves. Plan B is dead. It's also freezing. Plan C is ambitious. The lord's manor. The rich had private rooms upstairs, with real beds and real curtains. You tiptoe up the stairs. A soft bed. Heavy curtains. A door that shuts. And a very large man holding a lamp. "What are you doing in the lord's bedroom?" "Looking at curtains." Your wife nods like an expert. You are removed by the collar. Curtains, it turns out, are for people who own the building. Plan C is dead. Plan D. Wait. Everyone sleeps eventually. Your father snores. Your brothers sleep in a heap. Your mother finally turns away. And the grandmother's eyes glint in the dark. Your wife leans in and whispers a question. What day is it? You tell her. Her face falls. A church tradition, going back centuries, frowned on couples being together on certain days. How strictly people kept it is debated. Your grandmother keeps it strictly. For everyone. Plan D is dead. You're starting to feel like a guest in your own marriage. And that's when your wife says it. "Then stop looking for a room." Plan E. You build one. Planks. A saw. Your wife holding the board, saying you're doing it wrong. A bed with four walls and a door on the front. People really did this. A bed with walls and a door, a tiny house inside the house. They're called box beds. You climb in. Your wife follows, laughing into her sleeve so nobody hears. It's small in there. Her shoulder against yours. Her breath an inch from your ear. One inch of wood away, your father coughs. Your grandmother turns over, very pointedly. So you learn the first rule of marriage in a one-room house. Quiet is a skill. She finds your hand in the dark. And you find out she's been practicing longer than you. You pull the door. It sticks. Then it shuts. The firelight shrinks to one thin gold line. The house falls away. For the first time all day, nobody is watching. It wasn't much of a door. It was the only one in the house, and you built it yourself.
