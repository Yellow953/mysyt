# Story hunt H1: Money, power and hubris
Date: 2026-11-03 · Hunter: H1 · Seam: billionaires, kings, con men, schemes, absurd rulers

Method: WebSearch + WebFetch. Each fact below comes from a source I fetched or saw quoted in search results. "UNVERIFIED" marks anything from a single weak source or that I could not confirm. YouTube saturation was checked with a YouTube-only web search. That shows which videos exist, **not their view counts**, so the size estimates are rough.

Rejected during the hunt: **Salad Oil Scandal (Tino De Angelis, 1963).** It's a great story, but there are already 10+ English videos ("The Man Who Borrowed $180 Million Against Water", "He Filled Tanks With Seawater...", "The $60 Million Lie That Made Warren Buffett Rich"). It's saturated. Also skipped as too familiar: Gregor MacGregor/Poyais, Timothy Dexter, Emperor Norton, the Great Diamond Hoax, Tichborne.

## 1. The Safe With Nothing In It (Thérèse Humbert, France, 1879-1902)
- **One-sentence pitch:** A French farm girl borrowed roughly 50 million francs from Paris's top bankers for 20 years, all against a locked safe that nobody was allowed to open. When a court finally opened it, there was basically nothing inside.
- **Opening hook image:** A crowd in Paris (1902) watching officials crack open a giant iron safe that supposedly holds 100 million francs. Cut to what's inside.
- **Central conflict / escalation:** She invents a dying American millionaire, "Robert Henry Crawford," who she says left her his fortune after she helped him on a train in 1879. She says two "Crawford nephews" are contesting the will, so the bonds must stay locked in her safe until the lawsuit ends. Banks lend against the safe. She marries Frédéric Humbert, son of Gustave Humbert, later France's **Minister of Justice**, which makes her look untouchable. When lenders get nervous she pays them with new loans, then sets up a fake annuity/insurance fund (the "Rente Viagère") that takes in about 40 million francs to pay off old debts. Her own brothers play the "Crawford nephews" in court and keep the lawsuit going for years.
- **Biggest twist:** The lawsuit, the will and the nephews were all invented. Her brothers were the nephews, so she was suing herself to keep the safe shut.
- **Ending / payoff:** In 1902 a court orders the safe opened. The family flees and is caught in Madrid in December 1902. Thérèse and Frédéric get 5 years of hard labour each, and the brothers get 2 and 3 years. Thousands of small investors are ruined, and a banker named Girard in Elbeuf shot himself. Bonus: Henri Matisse's in-laws were caught up in the scandal (Hilary Spurling).
- **Sources:**
  - Wikipedia (cites Spurling, NYT 1903 trial coverage, Musée du Barreau de Paris): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th%C3%A9r%C3%A8se_Humbert
  - Evangeline Holland, Edwardian Promenade (cites Spurling, *La Grande Thérèse*, and B. F. Martin, *The Hypocrisy of Justice in the Belle Epoque*): https://edwardianpromenade.com/gossip/laffaire-humbert
  - Atlas Obscura article (could not fetch, 403; seen only in search snippet): https://atlasobscura.com/articles/who-was-therese-humbert-scammer
  - Book: Hilary Spurling, *La Grande Thérèse: The Greatest Swindle of the Century* (2000)
- **Disputed / legendary:** **What was in the safe is disputed.** Edwardian Promenade says a brick and an English halfpenny. Wikipedia and Atlas Obscura say old newspapers, a trouser button and an Italian coin. The script should say "almost nothing: some say a brick and a halfpenny, others a button and a coin." Loan totals differ: Holland gives 50M francs plus "nearly twice that again", and Wikipedia gives no total. Her death date is also disputed (Chicago 1918, or still alive in Paris in 1930).
- **YouTube saturation:** **Low in English.** The visible videos are French (Franck Ferrand, "L'histoire complètement dingue de la reine de l'escroquerie") plus one German. I found no clear English-language hit.
- **Length:** Fits 5 min well. The arc is simple: lie, safe, the lie grows, the safe opens.

## 2. The Man Who Ordered Real Money for Himself (Alves dos Reis, Portugal, 1925)
- **One-sentence pitch:** A Portuguese con man sat in a jail cell and worked out that he didn't need to forge money, just a contract. The Bank of Portugal's own official printer in London then printed him 100 million escudos in perfectly real banknotes.
- **Opening hook image:** A London print works stamping out genuine Portuguese banknotes, crate after crate, for a man who has no right to a single one.
- **Central conflict / escalation:** He already has a fake Oxford engineering diploma and has already used bad cheques to buy a railway company in Angola. Jailed for 54 days in 1924, he plans the big one. He forges a Bank of Portugal contract, has it notarised, gets it certified through embassies and forges the signatures of bank officials. Waterlow & Sons, which already holds the real plates, prints 200,000 notes of 500 escudos. He launders the money, founds his own bank (Banco Angola & Metrópole, June 1925), and then **tries to buy control of the Bank of Portugal itself** to cover his tracks. He gets about 10,000 of the 45,000 shares he needs.
- **Biggest twist:** The notes aren't fakes. They're real notes, so nobody can tell them apart. He's caught only because he re-used **serial numbers** that already existed.
- **Ending / payoff:** The newspaper *O Século* exposes it on 5 Dec 1925 and he is arrested days later. Sentenced in 1930 to 20 years. The scandal helped topple Portugal's First Republic in the 28 May 1926 coup, which led to the Salazar dictatorship (Estado Novo, 1926-1974). In 1932 Waterlow lost in the House of Lords and paid £610,392. One con man's fraud ends a democracy and a 150-year-old printing firm.
- **Sources:**
  - Wikipedia (cites Bloom 1962, Bull 1997, Armstrong-Fleming 2007, *Banco de Portugal v Waterlow* [1932] AC 452): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alves_dos_Reis
  - British Historical Society of Portugal, Andrew Bull (1997): https://www.bhsportugal.org/library/articles/alves-reis-and-the-portuguese-bank-note-scandal-of-1925
  - Spink Insider: https://insider.spink.com/?p=2694
  - Book: Murray Teigh Bloom, *The Man Who Stole Portugal* (1966)
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** The size is given as about 0.88% of GDP (Wikipedia) or "16.5% of notes in circulation" (Bull/BHS). Use a careful figure. Historians **debate how much** the scandal "caused" the 1926 coup. Say "helped bring down", not "caused".
- **YouTube saturation:** **Moderate.** There are at least 3-4 English videos ("Ocean's Portugal: The Heist That Ruined A Country", "The Man Who Nearly Destroyed Portugal's Economy", "The Craziest Historical Event You've Never Heard Of"), plus PT/FR/AR versions and at least one Short. I could not see view counts. We'd need a fresh angle, for example "he didn't fake money, he faked the paperwork".
- **Length:** Fits 5 min if we cut the Angola railway backstory to one line. It could easily run 8+ min.

## 3. The Fake Lord Who Robbed Jay Gould and Nearly Started a US-Canada War (Lord Gordon-Gordon, 1868-1874)
- **One-sentence pitch:** A fake Scottish lord swindled Jay Gould, the most ruthless robber baron in America, out of about a million dollars in railroad stock. When he fled to Canada, a posse that included future US congressmen tried to kidnap him back and nearly started an international incident.
- **Opening hook image:** A gang of Minnesota businessmen dragging a man in a plaid coat towards the US border at night, then Canadian officials stopping them about 100 yards short.
- **Central conflict / escalation:** It escalates in three steps. **(1) 1868:** as "Lord Glencairn" he fleeces London/Edinburgh jewellers (Wikipedia: £25,000, Marshall & Son). **(2) 1871:** in Minneapolis he convinces the Northern Pacific Railroad he's about to buy a huge acreage to settle Scottish tenants, and they wine and dine him. **(3) 1872, New York:** he convinces Gould he controls 60,000 Erie Railway shares and can help him in his fight for the Erie. Gould hands over about $1M in stock "as a pooling of interests" and Gordon sells it. Gould sues, Gordon skips $37,000 bail and flees to Manitoba.
- **Biggest twist:** The hunter becomes the hunted. Gould's side sends kidnappers into a foreign country, and **they** are the ones arrested (Pembina, 3 July 1873). Officials from Manitoba, Ottawa, Minnesota, Washington and London argue all summer, and Minnesota's governor reportedly readies the militia.
- **Ending / payoff:** In 1874, an Edinburgh jeweller he had swindled six years earlier finally tracks him down. When police arrive at his boarding house in Headingley to arrest him for extradition, he shoots himself (1 Aug 1874). His real name may have been John Crowningsfield, but that is uncertain.
- **Sources:**
  - Dictionary of Canadian Biography, "Gordon, Lord Gordon", by J. A. Bovey: https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/gordon_gordon_10E.html
  - Manitoba Historical Society, Memorable Manitobans (cites Bumsted, *Dictionary of Manitoba Biography*; Johnston, MHS Transactions 1950-51; David Samuels, *The New Yorker*, 1999): https://mhs.mb.ca/docs/people/gordongordon.shtml
  - Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Gordon_Gordon
- **Disputed / unverified:** The amounts are inconsistent. "About $1M" is the common figure, and one secondary site says $200,000 cash plus $1M stock (UNVERIFIED). His real identity is unknown. Wikipedia says the North-West Mounted Police made the arrests. **This is doubtful:** the NWMP was only formed in 1873, and the DCB just says the men were arrested at Pembina. Don't use it. The "Minnesota governor readied the militia" / near-war framing comes from Wikipedia. I did not see it in the DCB, so it is UNVERIFIED and needs checking before the script uses it.
- **YouTube saturation:** **Low-moderate.** There is a handful of videos ("A Con So Nice, He Named Himself Twice", a Minnesota History Theatre musical promo, podcast-style uploads, one Spanish). None looked like a major channel, but I could not see view counts.
- **Length:** Fits 5 min, and the three-con escalation suits it.

## 4. The Sugar Heir Who Crowned Himself Emperor of the Sahara (Jacques Lebaudy, 1903-1919)
- **One-sentence pitch:** A French sugar-fortune heir sailed his yacht to the coast of Africa, declared himself "Jacques I, Emperor of the Sahara", left his sailors to be captured by locals, refused to pay their ransom, and ran his "empire" from a London hotel.
- **Opening hook image:** A man in a top hat planting a flag on an empty beach and shouting "Sire!" at five confused sailors.
- **Central conflict / escalation:** He inherited a huge share of the Lebaudy sugar fortune (Wikipedia: 227M francs split among four children). In June 1903 he lands near Cape Juby with the yacht *Frasquita*, names a "Bay of Justice/Free Exchange" and a capital, "Troja", and issues stamps and coins. He leaves five sailors to hold his empire. They're captured and ransomed, he **refuses to pay**, and France sends a warship to rescue them. The sailors sue him. He sets up a "court" at London's Savoy Hotel with a "Governor-General", and France tries to draft him into the army. Later he moves to Long Island, where he's committed to an asylum (1915), escapes and is recaptured.
- **Biggest twist:** The "emperor" was a genuinely shrewd investor. He reportedly made over $1M on Erie stock in 1907 while acting like a madman.
- **Ending / payoff:** On 11 Jan 1919 his wife shoots him dead at their Long Island mansion, and a grand jury refuses to indict her. Accounts of why differ: he was trying to burn the house, or he threatened their daughter. Dark, so we'd keep it brief and non-graphic.
- **Sources:**
  - Wikipedia (cites Di Folco, *L'Empereur du Sahara* 2014; Bennet Maxwell, *The Independent* 15 Sep 1998; NYT 1915 and 1919; NY Daily News 2011): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Lebaudy
  - Long Island Herald, Dr Scott Eckers (2019): https://liherald.com/stories/phoenix-lodge-and-jacques-the-first-emperor-of-all-the-saharas,116654
  - Flags of the World, Empire of the Sahara: https://fotw.info/FLAGS/eh-emp03.html
  - Photo12 archive photo caption, "French warship rescues survivors from the Empire of the Sahara expedition, 1903": https://www.photo12.com/en/image/arp23a10_441
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** The number of sailors left behind is 5 or 8 depending on the source. Wikipedia's "400 hired soldiers and 16 Hotchkiss guns" is marked "reportedly", so treat it as UNVERIFIED. The reason for the shooting is disputed. I could not read the original NYT pieces.
- **YouTube saturation:** **Low.** I found only French videos ("Jacques Ier : l'empereur du Sahara", a book review) and no English one.
- **Length:** Fits 5 min. The Sahara-plus-hotel-court part is the core. The Long Island years can be the epilogue.

## 5. The Unemployed Shoemaker Who Conquered a Town With a Second-Hand Uniform (Wilhelm Voigt, "Captain of Köpenick", 1906)
- **One-sentence pitch:** A broke ex-convict shoemaker bought a used army captain's uniform from junk shops, ordered ten real soldiers to follow him, arrested a town's mayor, took the treasury, and the Kaiser laughed so hard he pardoned him.
- **Opening hook image:** A shabby man in an ill-fitting Prussian captain's coat barking orders at a squad of real soldiers, who snap to attention.
- **Central conflict / escalation:** He has spent about 30 years in prison. He's stuck in a classic catch-22: no papers without a job, no job without papers. On 16 Oct 1906 he puts on the uniform, takes command of about 10 soldiers, rides the train to Köpenick, seizes the town hall, arrests Mayor Georg Langerhans over "irregularities in the sewage works fund" and has the treasurer hand over the cash.
- **Biggest twist:** He reportedly wanted **a passport**, the paperwork that would let him live. But Köpenick's town hall had no passport office, so he left with about 3,557 marks instead. The whole raid worked only because Prussians obeyed the uniform without question.
- **Ending / payoff:** Arrested 10 days later and sentenced to 4 years. Petitions for clemency came from abroad, and the Kaiser pardoned him in August 1908. He then became a celebrity: autobiography, postcards, circus tours "as the Captain". Germany still uses the word *Köpenickiade* for this kind of con. It's the perfect "worst deal turns into best deal" ending.
- **Sources:**
  - Derek Scally, *The Irish Times*, 13 Oct 2006: https://www.irishtimes.com/news/from-cobbler-to-celebrity-by-donning-a-captain-s-uniform-1.1015108
  - HistoryNet: https://www.historynet.com/captain-of-kopenick/
  - Museum of Hoaxes: https://hoaxes.org/archive/permalink/the_captain_of_koepenick
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** The amount is 3,557.45 marks (Irish Times) or "over 4,000" (HistoryNet). The passport motive is the traditional explanation, partly shaped by Zuckmayer's 1931 play. Present it as "he later said / legend says". Irish Times says he was buried with full military honours after a French officer mistook him for someone important (single source, UNVERIFIED). The "Kaiser laughed" anecdote is "reportedly" in every source.
- **YouTube saturation:** **Moderate in German** (full 1931 and 1956 films, school-film clips, Shorts). **Low in English:** a couple of uploads ("The Homeless Who Captured a German Town Using a Trick That Cost Eighteen Marks", a podcast). I could not see view counts.
- **Length:** Fits 5 min comfortably. This is the best fit for our character: a lazy loser who gets the worst deal (no papers), then the best one.

## 6. The Homeless Teenager Germany's Aristocrats Treated as the Kaiser's Grandson (Harry Domela, 1926)
- **One-sentence pitch:** In 1926 a penniless, homeless 21-year-old let Germany's monarchist elite believe he was the exiled Kaiser's grandson. They fed and housed him in luxury across Thuringia, and after his arrest he wrote a bestseller and starred as himself in the movie.
- **Opening hook image:** A town mayor and officials in top hats bowing at a railway station to a skinny young man in a borrowed suit.
- **Central conflict / escalation:** He's a Baltic German war orphan who fought in a Freikorps at 15, ended up homeless and had petty convictions for impostures. In Heidelberg he poses as "Prince Lieven" with titled students. People notice he looks like Prince Wilhelm of Prussia, and a rumour spreads that he's the Kaiser's grandson travelling incognito. He never quite says it, he just stops denying it. In Erfurt/Weimar/Gotha, monarchists who hate the Weimar Republic compete to host him, and he lives in hotels without paying.
- **Biggest twist:** **He reportedly never asked for money** (Wikipedia). The elite paid to fool themselves because they wanted the monarchy back. The con exposed them, not him.
- **Ending / payoff:** He panics at the press attention and tries to run off to the French Foreign Legion, but is arrested boarding the train (Jan 1927). In Cologne prison he writes *Der falsche Prinz* (1927), which becomes a bestseller, then plays himself in the 1927 film and buys a Berlin cinema to screen it. Later he fled the Nazis, fought in the Spanish Civil War, and died as an art teacher in Venezuela in 1979.
- **Sources:**
  - Deutsche Biographie (NDB/ADB, Bavarian Academy): https://www.deutsche-biographie.de/gnd118526618.html
  - Wikipedia (cites NYT 1927, Literaturland Thüringen, Jens Kirsten research): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Domela
  - Der Freitag, "1926: Königliche Hoheit": https://www.freitag.de/autoren/behrang-samsami/1926-koenigliche-hoheit
- **Disputed:** **The verdict conflicts.** Wikipedia says he was acquitted. Deutsche Biographie (the more authoritative source) says 7 months for fraud, offset by time already served. A 1927 newspaper also reports 7 months. Use "7 months, already served". Deutsche Biographie says the bestseller was ghost-written and reworked by publishers and that Domela disputed it. That's a nice extra twist, but handle it carefully. "Never took money" is from Wikipedia only. Deutsche Biographie says he lived in hotels without paying, so phrase it as "never asked for cash".
- **YouTube saturation:** **Very low.** One French video. Searches in English are swamped by Prince Harry content.
- **Length:** Fits 5 min. There's a risk the middle section feels samey (more dinners, more bowing), so it needs one or two specific scenes. I could not verify concrete dinner/parade details beyond the hotel stays. Research needed.

## 7. The Country That Put Its Whole Savings Into Pyramid Schemes (Albania, 1996-97)
- **One-sentence pitch:** Just out of communism, about two-thirds of Albanians put their savings into "investment companies" that by the end promised to double or triple their money in a few months. When the schemes collapsed, the government fell, the army's armouries were looted and around 2,000 people died.
- **Opening hook image:** An ordinary man selling his apartment and his cow to hand the cash across a desk for a receipt promising 30% a month.
- **Central conflict / escalation:** The rates climb: 4-5% a month, then 6%, then 8% before the May 1996 election. Sude offers 12-19% a month, Populli over 30%. By November, Xhafferi promises to treble deposits in three months. Liabilities reach **nearly half of GDP**. The biggest firms were believed to be funded by smuggling into Yugoslavia during UN sanctions, and that income dried up when sanctions were lifted. The president defended the companies, officials attended their events, and several firms donated to the ruling party. The IMF warned and was ignored.
- **Biggest twist:** The government didn't stop it. It helped it look legitimate. When it fell, the people who had lost everything raided the army depots.
- **Ending / payoff:** Sude defaults on 19 Nov 1996. Riots break out in Jan 1997, the government loses the south and resigns in March, UN-backed peacekeepers arrive in April and the president resigns later in 1997. About 2,000 dead. The lek loses 40%.
- **Sources:**
  - Christopher Jarvis, IMF *Finance & Development* (March 2000), "The Rise and Fall of Albania's Pyramid Schemes": https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2000/03/jarvis.htm
  - Wikipedia, 1997 Albanian civil unrest: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_Albanian_civil_unrest
- **Disputed:** The death toll is about 2,000 (IMF), and some sources go up to about 3,800. Whether it was a "civil war" is debated, so say "the country collapsed into armed chaos". The "sold his apartment" hook is a typical example, not a documented person, so it would have to be framed as illustrative. **Tone risk:** this is real mass death in recent history, so the sarcastic mascot has to punch at the scheme operators and politicians, never the victims.
- **YouTube saturation:** **Moderate.** There are existing explainers ("The Pyramid Scheme That Ruined A Country", "The Country That Bet Its Life Savings on a Lie") plus news archive footage. I could not see view counts.
- **Length:** It needs more than 5 min to do justice. At 5 min we'd cut it down to the rates going up and then the collapse.

## 8. The Man Who Was a British MP, a German Spy, a Nazi Contact and a Buddhist Abbot (Ignaz Trebitsch-Lincoln, 1879-1943)
- **One-sentence pitch:** A Hungarian petty thief became, in order, a Christian missionary, a Church of England curate, a British Member of Parliament, a German double agent, a jailed fraudster, the press censor in a German coup, an adviser to Chinese warlords, a Buddhist abbot in Shanghai, and a self-proclaimed Dalai Lama.
- **Opening hook image:** A Buddhist monk in robes in 1930s Shanghai, with a flashback: the same face in a top hat in the House of Commons.
- **Central conflict / escalation:** Each reinvention is bigger than the last. He's elected Liberal MP for Darlington in Jan 1910 and loses the seat the same year. He fails at oil deals in Bucharest. Britain turns down his offer to spy, so he offers himself to Germany. He flees to the US in 1915 and sells his story to the *New York World*. He's extradited for **fraud** (not spying), serves 3 years in Parkhurst and is deported (1919). In Berlin's Kapp Putsch (1920) he becomes the coup's press censor and meets Hitler. He sells secrets, then goes to China to work for warlords, becomes abbot "Chao Kung" (1931), and after the 13th Dalai Lama died, proclaims **himself** Dalai Lama.
- **Biggest twist:** The man who betrayed every side ends up as a "holy man". Darker beat: his son was hanged for murder in England in 1926.
- **Ending / payoff:** He dies in Shanghai in 1943. Rumours that he was poisoned are UNVERIFIED.
- **Sources:**
  - Wikipedia (cites Wasserstein, NYT 8 May 1988): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignaz_Trebitsch-Lincoln
  - History Today, "Chasing a Chameleon: Trebitsch Lincoln": https://www.historytoday.com/archive/chasing-chameleon-trebitsch-lincoln
  - Encyclopedia.com entry: https://encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/lincoln-trebitsch
  - Book: Bernard Wasserstein, *The Secret Lives of Trebitsch Lincoln* (Yale UP, 1988), the standard scholarly biography
- **Disputed:** The Wikipedia article is tagged "needs more citations". His own memoir (*Revelations of an International Spy*, 1916) is self-serving. The Hitler meeting, the Dalai Lama claim and the Nazi offers should all be checked against Wasserstein. The poisoning story is rumour only.
- **YouTube saturation:** **Moderate.** There are at least 3 English videos ("The Jewish, Presbyterian, Buddhist, Nazi Spy", "The Jewish Nazi Dalai Lama"), one French and one Short. I could not see view counts. Religious/ethnic framing in existing titles is a monetization/tone risk.
- **Length:** **Needs more than 5 min.** It's a list of reinventions rather than one escalating plot. At 5 min it becomes a montage. Better as a 3-act cut (MP, then spy/prison, then monk) or kept for a longer slot.

## Strongest 3 for our channel
1. **Thérèse Humbert's safe.** It has the cleanest single-object story (a locked safe), a clear escalation (more and more loans against it), a brilliant twist (she was suing herself through fake nephews) and a visual payoff (the safe opens). It's barely covered in English. Mr. Yellow Scarf could play a creditor staring into the empty safe.
2. **Captain of Köpenick.** It's the most "on-brand": a down-and-out nobody gets the worst deal (no papers), puts on a used uniform and accidentally conquers a town. The Kaiser laughs and he becomes famous. The ending is upbeat, it's monetization-safe and it's under-covered in English.
3. **Lord Gordon-Gordon vs Jay Gould.** A con man robs the ultimate robber baron, and then the kidnappers get arrested. Three escalating cons and an ending where a six-year-old jewellery swindle catches up with him. Before scripting, verify the militia/near-war claim in a second source.

Honourable mentions: **Lebaudy**, which has a great one-liner and almost no English coverage but a darker ending, and **Alves dos Reis**, the strongest story overall but already moderately covered on YouTube.
