## Head-to-head against the competitor premises
The breakout videos win on a question the stranger already has: "what if everyone vanished", "what did soldiers do all day", "how was the toilet invented". Lesson 1 in competitor-watch-v2 is that baseline demand did the work.

Honest limit: none of the four finalists is a pre-existing question. They are strong stories, not pre-demanded topics. The title and thumbnail therefore have to create the question in one line. That is a harder job than the competitors had, and it is the real risk across the whole set.

Köpenick comes closest ("could a costume fool soldiers?"). Kiska creates its question in four words, which is the lesson-2 contrast test. Reis and Humbert need the viewer to already care about 1925 Portuguese banking or a French safe. Nothing in the evidence says they do.

## D. Köpenick (rank 1)

**Cold click.** "He Bought a Used Uniform and Arrested a Mayor" works. It is concrete (used uniform), has a surprising verb (arrested a mayor), and implies a status inversion. Weakness: "a mayor" of where? Without Germany or soldiers the title is a bit small. A stranger may read "mayor" as a small-town prank. The strongest element, real soldiers obeying, is only in the alternates.
- Sharper: "Real Soldiers Obeyed a Man in a Second-Hand Uniform"
- Sharper: "He Bought a Used Captain's Uniform. Then the Army Followed Him."
- Keep as backup: "A Used Uniform Let Him Arrest a Mayor"

**One-sentence test.** Passes: an ex-con, a second-hand uniform, soldiers who follow orders, a town hall taken.

**Does the honest version survive?** Yes, and it is better. The legend (a desperate passport quest, the Kaiser laughing) was the weak part. The verified version says he planned it for weeks, the court disbelieved the passport story, and historians suspect money. That turns a "poor man's quest" into "the system obeys a coat", which is a sharper and more modern theme. It also makes him less lovable. The mascot is a lazy sarcastic everyman, so a slightly shifty schemer is on-brand, but the script must still give us a reason to root for him.

**Escalation.** Real and clean: buys the uniform, tests it on a squad in the street, takes more soldiers, train, town hall, mayor arrested, cash taken, receipt signed with his old warden's name, vanishes. The receipt signed with the warden's name (ideas-v2 D, step 5) is the best single beat in the whole set. It is only de.wikipedia plus a snippet, so verify it.

**Sag risks.**
1. The opening backstory (prison, no papers). The hook as written is good, but if the script goes "born 1849, shoemaker, jailed at 14" it dies. Open on the heist and backfill.
2. The back third (caught by a cellmate, sentenced, pardoned, celebrity). It is warm but diffuse, as the packager admits. The loop "the man who could not get papers became one of the most famous men in Germany" is the best ending available, and it is earned. Make sure "Köpenickiade" (a word entered the language) is the final beat, not the pardon.
3. The heist itself lasts about a day. Five minutes means about 2 minutes on the heist and a tight 1 minute of consequences. It holds only if we linger on "nobody asked for a single paper".

**Weakest point (packager):** the passport legend is mostly play-shaped. Does it kill it? No, the hedged "he said passport, the court did not believe it" version is more interesting.

**Saturation.** One 82K video 4 months old is proof of demand, not a wall. Plainly Difficult at 28K is 9 years old. The 1956 film at 748K shows the audience exists. This is the only finalist with demand evidence for the story itself.

**Stickman/5 min.** Best of the set. The tiny-coat-big-uniform gag is native to the mascot, and soldiers as identical faceless stick figures is cheap and funny.

**Tone/monetization.** Clean. Only fix: the mayor was not proven corrupt ("claimed irregularities", already noted).

**Thumbnail.** Tiny mascot in an oversized uniform, a line of soldiers saluting. "SALUTE" is okay. Sharper: "THEY SALUTED" (adds the shock), or "NO QUESTIONS". Avoid "NO PAPERS", which sells the legend we are trimming. Scarf worn over the uniform is a good brand moment.

## C. Kiska (rank 2)

**Cold click.** "34,000 Soldiers Invaded an Island. Nobody Was There." is the best title of the four. It has a number, a contradiction and a how. It is in the "Actually"/"NO FIGHTING" family. WW2 baseline demand is proven (Ink Story, 493K). The alternates are weaker; "518 Shells at Nothing" is good for a thumbnail, not a title.

**One-sentence test.** Passes: the Allies invaded an empty island because the enemy left 18 days earlier.

**Does the honest version hold?** The core ("nobody was there") is the most solid claim in the whole set. The soft parts are casualty totals, the cause of the Pips, and the friendly-fire split. None of these is the appeal. The appeal survives as long as the script does not sell the ending on a crisp body count. The verifier's own advice (the evidence was there and was explained away) makes the story better than the legend.

**Escalation.** Good, and it has dramatic irony. The viewer learns early that the island is empty while the Allies do not, so 18 days of shelling an empty island plays as tension plus comedy. The Pips (518 shells at nothing) is a bonus prologue. Do not imply the Pips were connected to the escape; two misreads is the honest framing.

**Sag risks.**
1. The hook line "And somehow, people still die" (ideas-v2 C hook) is a gear-shift that sours the comedy in the first 15 seconds. Decide the tone: absurd comedy that turns sober, and signal it once.
2. After the evacuation reveal, the middle is bombing an empty island. It is funny for about 30 seconds and then repeats. It needs the Japanese-side clock (the aborted first attempt, the fog) to keep a second storyline alive.
3. The ending. Soft casualty numbers plus real deaths plus "dogs" can go flat or sound mocking. The "stray dogs and a lot of mistakes" closing line is a loop but sits oddly beside about 300 casualties. A better close should return to the opening image (men hitting a beach in fog, the enemy not there) and land on the cost, not a quip.
4. The "nine thousand expected" line is single-source (Boomhower). Attribute or cut.

**Weakest point (packager):** soft numbers, tone, the AI-channel wave. Does it kill it? Soft numbers no (use ranges). Tone is manageable if the joke targets the planning and the radar. The AI wave is real: about 15 tiny uploads this quarter, mostly under 100 views, largest 1.4K. That is a warning about sameness, but those uploads show no winner, not that a good package cannot win. The package has to look nothing like them (no stock-art fog).

**Not yet done:** the verifier says to open Kostka (JFQ) and the NHHC narrative before scripting. Do that before Stage 2.

**Stickman/5 min.** Fine. Fog as white or grey backgrounds is cheap, though visual monotony is a risk. The radar-screen pips and a ship firing at blank sea are good gags. Keep the Japanese ships as silhouettes.

**Tone/monetization.** About 70 died on the Abner Read and perhaps 25 to 32 to friendly fire. No gore is needed, but the narration must never joke at the dead. Moderate risk; manageable.

**Thumbnail.** The soldier aiming at a tiny dog in fog is excellent. Text: "VS NOBODY" is awkward. Better: "NOBODY HOME" or just "0 ENEMIES". A number on a thumbnail follows lesson 5.

## A. Alves dos Reis (rank 3)

**Cold click.** Weak. "He Never Faked a Single Banknote" is a riddle in a negative form, and it is not strictly honest: he forged contracts, signatures and a diploma, and the notes were unauthorised printings. A viewer with ten other videos open will not parse "never faked" quickly. It is also a bit smug.
- Sharper: "The Bank's Own Printer Made His Money" (alternate 1, clearer and concrete)
- Sharper: "He Got the Bank's Printer to Print His Money"
- Sharper: "A Con Man Ordered Real Banknotes From the Bank's Own Printer" (alternate 2, long but plain)

**One-sentence test.** Passes, but the sentence is long and has a jargon load (escudos, Waterlow, serial numbers).

**Honest version.** Holds; verified claims are the strongest of the four (House of Lords case, Spink). The "both real" thumbnail concept is smart. But the pitch's many "word carefully" fixes (first order only, sentence length, coup link) show how much of the sheet is hedging. The coup link must be one sentence at most.

**Sag.** The packager's own weakness is correct. After the great hook (real notes, fake paperwork), the middle (forged contract, suitcases at the Ritz, laundering through a private bank, buying shares in the Bank of Portugal) is abstract finance with no visual or emotional beat. Worse, the climax is bureaucratic: duplicate serial numbers found by a money-changer's employee. That is an anticlimax after a "how can a note be both real and stolen?" promise. The best ending beats (the bank honours every note, and the printer pays about GBP 600,000 after a House of Lords case) are legal and statistical, not emotional.

**Emotion.** There is no one to care about. He is a skilled fraudster with no wound and no reversal for himself. The real emotional victim is Waterlow, who is a company.

**Saturation.** Moderate: 3 to 4 English videos. The premise reads like standard "genius fraud" content.

**Verdict.** The mechanism is good enough that I would not kill it. As a lead for Joe's "irresistible" bar it falls short.

## B. Humbert (rank 4)

**Cold click.** "The Safe Nobody Was Allowed to Open" is a decent mystery-box title. But it is also the most predictable-twist title: the viewer guesses "it's empty" within seconds. Packager's weakest point is correct and I think it is fatal as a lead. The reveal is the only twist, the title spoils it, and the verified contents are "almost nothing; accounts differ" (coin and button, or brick and halfpenny, or empty). A reveal we must hedge is a payoff we cannot deliver crisply.

**One-sentence test.** Passes.

**Honest version.** Weak. The amounts are soft (about 50 million, or nearly 100), the pyramid layer is one blog, her father-in-law's role is unresolved, her fate is unknown, and nearly all detail traces to one book we did not open. The flip ("her own brothers played the nephews") is strong, but the best-known phrasing ("she sued herself") is a dramatization that needs a flag.

**Sag.** Fifteen of the twenty years are "she borrows again". The Rente Viagère layer would give a second engine but is single-source. The ending (fled, caught in Madrid, five years, then she vanishes) is anticlimactic.

**Demand.** Low English competition does not mean demand. No English test exists. That is a gamble, not a edge.

**Where it is good.** The 10,000-person crowd at the safe, the wax seals, and the fake family are great visuals. The mascot as a polite creditor is perfect.

**Better title if kept:** "Paris Lent Her Millions for 20 Years. Nobody Checked the Safe." Thumbnail: "20 YEARS" over a huge safe. Keep as reserve only.

## Is the new set stronger than Fordlandia / Onoda / Bone Wars?
Yes, in three concrete ways, but not as much as the sell sheets suggest.

1. **Mechanism.** The rejected batch was topic-first with unverified facts (see the backlog header: "UNVERIFIED"). Fordlandia and Bone Wars were "rich man's slapstick fall" and "two rivals escalate" with no one-image hook; Onoda was a well-trod war story with civilian-death problems. The new set has a clear contrast per story (soldiers obey a costume, an invasion with no enemy) and a thumbnail image per story. That is a real upgrade.
2. **Facts.** Every claim has been through a verification pass, and the sheets carry honest "word carefully" lists. The earlier batch had none of this.
3. **Endings.** Each new story has a flip or loop ending decided up front (competitor lesson 4). The old batch did not.

Where it is not clearly stronger. Köpenick and Kiska are better than Fordlandia/Bone Wars. Reis and Humbert are about equal to them: good stories with weak clicks. All four are still "weird history" with no pre-existing demand, so the new set fixes story quality but only partly fixes the "predictable" complaint from Joe. A viewer who has seen weird-history channels may feel they know the genre: "con man", "military blunder", "ex-convict wins". The freshness has to come from the title and thumbnail execution and from the angle (obedience, misreading), not from the topics.

## Top problems (with locations)
1. **Premise demand gap.** ideas-v2 "Premise lessons applied" claims lesson 1 is satisfied, but the four finalists are not universal questions. Only Köpenick and Kiska make a stranger ask one by themselves.
2. **Humbert's payoff is unstatable.** ideas-v2 B "Biggest twist" and "Weakest point" versus _verify-humbert #17 (contents DISPUTED). The twist is hedge-only and the title spoils it.
3. **Reis's title and climax.** ideas-v2 A "Proposed title" is a riddle and not fully accurate; "Biggest twist" is a clerical duplicate-serial discovery. The middle is finance.
4. **Kiska's tone and soft close.** ideas-v2 C "Opening hook" last line ("somehow, people still die") and "Ending / payoff" closing joke beside about 300 casualties. Also the Kostka/NHHC sources are still unopened (_verify-kiska "Research limitations").
5. **Köpenick's motive and receipt detail.** ideas-v2 D "Biggest twist" plus _verify-kopenick #12, #14. The honest motive makes him less lovable, and the best beat (the warden's name on the receipt) rests on one source. Verify before building the story around it.

## Genuinely strong
- Köpenick hook (ideas-v2 D opening) and the receipt beat; the "obedience is the antagonist" frame; "Köpenickiade" as a closing fact.
- Kiska title; the dramatic irony of an 18-day bombardment of an empty island; the Pips as prologue.
- Reis's printer-pays reversal and the "both real" thumbnail concept.
- The honesty discipline: every sheet lists what not to say. This is better than the rejected batch.

## Keep researching or present?
Present two (Köpenick, Kiska) and say plainly that a third did not clear the bar. If Joe wants a third, there is a candidate already in the folder that outranks Humbert and Reis on cold-click: **Bunton** (_verify-bunton.md). "He Stole a Goya to Pay for Pensioners' TV Licences" is a clear, sympathetic, universal premise with an ending twist (acquitted of the painting, convicted of the frame; Dr. No). Caveats: a 132K competitor (Lume) already covers the son twist, and the "son did it" claim is unproven, so it must be told as "Bunton always said he acted alone; in 1969 his son told police he took it". It deserves a quick sell sheet before any further hunt. The groundnut scheme (_verify-groundnut.md) is the same "bureaucratic fiasco" genre as the rejected Fordlandia and I would not revive it.
