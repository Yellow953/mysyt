## 1. The man who got bumped and changed every flight (airline overbooking)
- **Pillar:** 5 (how a business really makes money). This is Joe's direction.
- **Working title:** "Airlines Sell Seats That Don't Exist. One Angry Passenger Took Them to the Supreme Court"
- **Alt title:** "Why Your Airline Sells Your Seat Twice"
- **One-sentence premise:** Airlines quietly sell more tickets than seats on purpose, and the story of how that became legal, then paid, then a scandal runs through a famous consumer crusader, an economist everyone laughed at, and a doctor dragged off a plane.
- **Opening-hook image:** Mr. Yellow Scarf walks up to the gate holding a ticket marked "CONFIRMED – OK". He finds his seat already taken by a stranger, and 107 people are standing around a plane with 100 seats.
- **Characters:**
  - **Ralph Nader:** the most famous consumer advocate in America, bumped in 1972.
  - **Julian Simon:** an economist who proposed in 1968 that airlines should *pay* people to give up their seats, and was ignored.
  - **Alfred Kahn:** the regulator who finally let airlines do it.
  - **Dr. David Dao:** dragged off United Express 3411 in 2017.
- **Central conflict and escalation:**
  1. The money mechanic. Empty seats are lost money, and on average some passengers do not show up, so airlines sell extra tickets.
  2. April 28, 1972. Nader has a confirmed seat on Allegheny flight 864 (Washington to Hartford) to speak at a noon rally. He arrives five minutes before departure and is told that all seats are taken. Allegheny had confirmed 107 reservations for 100 seats. He refuses the $32.41 compensation and sues.
  3. A jury awards him $10 in compensatory damages and $25,000 in punitive damages. The appeals court reverses that. The case reaches the Supreme Court, which rules for Nader in 1976 (*Nader v. Allegheny Airlines*, 426 U.S. 290).
  4. Meanwhile, Simon's 1968 idea is a reverse auction: ask who will give up their seat for the least money. It was considered faintly absurd and was not used. After Kahn became CAB chair in 1977, airlines were allowed to try it (around 1978).
  5. Overbooking becomes routine. Then, in April 2017, United offers at most $800, nobody volunteers, and police drag Dr. Dao off the plane. The video goes global.
- **Twist:** The "fix" that makes bumping almost painless (paying volunteers) was a decades-old idea from one economist, and the industry needed a lawsuit and then a viral video before it took it seriously. After Dao, Delta lets supervisors offer up to $9,950 and United raises its cap to $10,000.
- **Payoff:** Overbooking never stopped, because it is pure math. What changed is who pays for the math. The audience leaves knowing how to get paid when they are bumped.
- **Key verifiable figures/dates:**
  - Flight 864, April 28, 1972, 107 confirmed for 100 seats, $32.41 offered, $25,000 punitive award, Supreme Court 1976. All from the Supreme Court opinion.
  - Simon, "An Almost Practical Solution to Airline Overbooking," *Journal of Transport Economics and Policy* 2(2), May 1968.
  - Kahn became CAB chair in 1977.
  - Dao incident April 2017, $800 maximum offer before removal, settlement terms undisclosed.
  - United's $10,000 cap and Delta's $9,950 in 2017.
  - In Q1 2023 there were about 5,300 involuntary bumps, and Delta had zero.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/426/290 (opinion with the full facts)
  - https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-426/pdf/USREPORTS-426-290.pdf
  - https://www.independent.org/tir/2023-fall/julian-simon/ (Simon's 1968 paper, Kahn 1977)
  - https://fee.org/articles/delta-paid-passengers-10-000-to-give-up-their-flight-seats-how-one-economist-made-it-possible/
  - https://conversableeconomist.blogspot.com/2017/04/julian-simons-almost-practical-solution.html
  - https://www.cbsnews.com/sanfrancisco/news/united-airlines-dragging-dr-david-dao-settlement/
  - https://www.fox13news.com/news/united-raising-limit-on-payments-to-bumped-flyers-to-10000
  - Simon's own account: https://cato.org/regulation/spring-1994/origins-airline-oversales-auction-system (returned 403 here; it needs reading)
- **Disputed / handle with care:**
  - It is *not verified* that the Nader case directly caused the auction system. The two run in parallel, so narrate them as parallel threads and do not claim causation.
  - The exact 1978 CAB rule wording is not yet confirmed. Simon's 1994 Regulation article should confirm it.
  - Nader's final outcome after the 1976 remand needs checking.
  - The Dao settlement amount is undisclosed, so never state a figure.
  - Nader is a living public figure, so keep him factual and not mocked.
- **Saturation:** "Why airlines overbook" is well covered as an *explainer*: Wendover Productions, "Why Airlines Sell More Seats Than They Have," about 2.3M views (aggregator figure), plus a widely shared TED-Ed lesson. I found no big video built as a *story* around Nader, Simon and Dao. The angle is fresh, but the topic is known.
- **Money visual (plain language):** A plane drawn as 100 seats, with 107 little ticket-holders queuing.
  - A dice roll shows that about 7 of them do not usually show up.
  - Each empty seat burns a dollar bill.
  - The auction is Mr. Yellow Scarf holding up a sign: "I'll leave for $200… $400… $800!"
- **~5-minute fit:** Good. There are three acts (1972 lawsuit, 1968 idea becomes 1978 system, 2017 explosion) plus a 30-second payoff on viewer rights.

## 2. McDonald's is a landlord that happens to sell burgers
- **Pillar:** 5 (how a business really makes money, plus a rivalry). This is Joe's direction.
- **Working title:** "McDonald's Doesn't Really Sell Burgers. It's Your Landlord"
- **Alt title:** "The Man Who Turned McDonald's Into a Real-Estate Empire"
- **One-sentence premise:** Ray Kroc was going broke on the franchises he sold, until his finance man realised the money was in the *dirt under the restaurant*, not the burgers. Today rent is McDonald's biggest franchise income.
- **Opening-hook image:** A giant McDonald's sign. Mr. Yellow Scarf lifts it and finds a "FOR RENT – PAY MONTHLY" sign behind it.
- **Characters:**
  - **Dick and Mac McDonald:** inventors of the Speedee system who did not want to grow.
  - **Ray Kroc:** a milkshake-machine salesman turned franchising agent with thin margins.
  - **Harry J. Sonneborn:** the finance man behind the real-estate model, whom almost nobody knows.
- **Central conflict and escalation:**
  1. Kroc's franchise deal paid him only a small cut of sales, and he was barely making money.
  2. Sonneborn's model, run through Franchise Realty Corp. in 1956, has the company control the land and building and charge the franchisee rent, with rent rising alongside sales. That gives them leverage, because a franchisee who breaks the rules loses the lease.
  3. The brothers keep blocking Kroc's changes. In 1961 Kroc buys them out for $2.7 million. Sonneborn raises the money, and the figure was reportedly sized so each brother got $1M after tax.
  4. Kroc and Sonneborn later clash, and Sonneborn leaves in 1967.
- **Twist:** The real genius was not Kroc, and the "real estate" line everyone quotes is from a movie (*The Founder*, 2016), not a documented Sonneborn quote. Our version can say this honestly: "the line is Hollywood's, but the numbers are real."
- **Payoff:** McDonald's FY2024 10-K:
  - About 95% of its 43,477 restaurants are franchised.
  - Franchised revenue was rent $10,017M versus royalties $5,606M.
  - It owns about 56% of the land and about 80% of the buildings in its consolidated markets.

  So rent is nearly double the royalties: the burgers pay the rent.
- **Key verifiable figures/dates:**
  - 10-K figures above.
  - 1961 buyout at $2.7M.
  - Franchise Realty Corp. from 1956. Its exact name varies by source.
  - Sonneborn president roughly 1959-1967.
  - The primary book is John F. Love, *McDonald's: Behind the Arches* (1986/1995).
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/63908/000006390825000012/mcd-20241231.htm (FY2024 10-K)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_J._Sonneborn (cites Love)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_and_Maurice_McDonald
  - https://qz.com/965779/mcdonalds-isnt-really-a-fast-food-chain-its-a-brilliant-30-billion-real-estate-company
  - https://www.restfinance.com/restaurant-finance-across-america/ray-kroc-not-the-founder-but-a-financial-engineer/article_a3d94b01-60db-5a81-9ac9-6e1ec10d8d28.html
- **Disputed:**
  - The handshake royalty (said to be 0.5% or 1%) rests only on a McDonald nephew's claim, with no documents. Flag it as legend or leave it out.
  - The "not technically in the food business" quote is UNVERIFIED (low-quality sources only).
  - "Brothers betrayed": Dick McDonald reportedly said he had no regrets. Do not present betrayal as fact.
  - "Rent > royalties" is solid, but "McDonald's makes most of its *profit* from real estate" is a simplification, because the 10-K reports revenue, not profit by source.
- **Saturation:** HIGH.
  - PolyMatter, "How McDonald's Really Makes Money," about 4M views.
  - The Food Theorists covered it.
  - *The Founder* film exists.
  - We would need the Sonneborn-as-hidden-hero angle plus the "the famous quote is fake" twist to stand out.
- **Money visual:** Two piggy banks next to a burger. The "royalties" pig gets a thin coin stream and the "rent" pig gets a fat one, scaled 5.6 to 10.0. A franchisee stickman pays rent to a giant golden landlord.
- **~5-minute fit:** Good, if we skip the full Kroc biography and stay on Sonneborn's decision.

## 3. The $1.50 hot dog: Costco makes its money before you walk in
- **Pillar:** 5 (how a business really makes money). This is Joe's direction.
- **Working title:** "Costco's CEO Wanted to Raise the Hot Dog Price. The Founder Said 'I'll Kill You'"
- **Alt title:** "Costco Doesn't Make Money Selling You Stuff"
- **One-sentence premise:** Costco's hot dog combo has cost $1.50 since 1985 and the company refused to raise it through decades of inflation, because Costco's real product is the membership card in your wallet.
- **Opening-hook image:** A $1.50 price tag frozen in a block of ice while calendar pages fly past (1985… 2008… 2022…) and every other price balloons.
- **Characters:**
  - **Jim Sinegal:** co-founder.
  - **Craig Jelinek:** CEO 2012-2023, the one who wanted to raise the price.
  - **Ron Vachris:** the current CEO, who said "No" in one word in 2024.
- **Central conflict and escalation:**
  1. The hot dog combo has lost money for years.
  2. Jelinek asks to raise the price, and Sinegal (as Jelinek recounts it): "If you raise the effing hot dog, I will kill you. Figure it out."
  3. Costco "figures it out" by bringing hot dog production in-house.
  4. Then comes the bigger secret. Costco caps its markups and sells nearly everything near cost, so where does the money come from?
- **Twist:** In FY2025 membership fees were $5.323B against net income of $8.099B, so fees equal about two-thirds of Costco's entire profit. The renewal rate was 92.3% in the US and Canada. The hot dog is bait for a subscription.
- **Payoff:** "Every time you renew your card, you're paying for Costco's profit. The hot dog is the thank-you note."
- **Key verifiable figures/dates:**
  - FY2025 10-K and press release (membership fees, net income, renewal rate).
  - $1.50 since 1985.
  - Jelinek's anecdote as reported by Fortune.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/909832/000090983225000101/cost-20250831.htm
  - https://investor.costco.com/news/news-details/2025/Costco-Wholesale-Corporation-Reports-Fourth-Quarter-and-Fiscal-Year-2025-Operating-Results/default.aspx
  - https://fortune.com/2024/06/01/costco-ceo-hot-dog-price-story
  - https://www.nbclosangeles.com/news/business/money-report/costco-ceos-one-word-answer-to-whether-he-would-raise-the-price-of-hot-dogs-no/2934880/
  - https://www.fool.com/investing/2025/12/05/how-costco-makes-money-despite-150-hot-dog-combos
- **Disputed / care:**
  - Sinegal's line exists in slightly different wordings. Quote it as "as Jelinek tells it."
  - "Fees = two-thirds of profit" is a comparison (fees ÷ net income), not an accounting split. Phrase it that way.
  - The markup cap (often quoted as 14-15%) is reported widely but UNVERIFIED in filings. Use "reportedly."
  - The hot-dog-loss figure is unknown. Do not invent one.
  - The year of in-house production still needs checking.
- **Saturation:** VERY HIGH. "How Costco makes money" and "the $1.50 hot dog" are evergreen topics with many videos and articles. I could not get exact view counts. The story is weaker on rivalry: there is a conflict, but it is soft.
- **Money visual:** A Costco profit pie. The thin "selling stuff" slice is tiny and the "membership cards" slice is about 2/3. Mr. Yellow Scarf eats a hot dog while a hand quietly takes $65 from his wallet.
- **~5-minute fit:** Medium. The story beats are thin and the material is more explainer than story. It would be stronger as a section of a "secret business model" video, or with a rival (Sam's Club / Sol Price, Sinegal's mentor) added for conflict.

## 4. "Two free flights!": the vacuum promotion that ate Hoover
- **Pillar:** 5 (collapse from a terrible decision; lesser-known for US viewers)
- **Working title:** "Buy a £100 Vacuum, Get £600 of Free Flights. What Could Go Wrong?"
- **Alt title:** "The Worst Sales Promotion in History"
- **One-sentence premise:** In 1992 Hoover UK promised two free return flights to anyone who bought £100 of its products, the British public did the maths faster than Hoover did, and the company lost its executives, its money and eventually its UK business.
- **Opening-hook image:** Mr. Yellow Scarf buys a vacuum cleaner, tosses it in a bin without opening the box, and runs off with plane tickets.
- **Characters:**
  - **Hoover UK management:** they planned on few people redeeming.
  - **Two angry customers:** Harry Cichy and Sandy Jack, who form the "Hoover Holiday Pressure Group."
  - **Maytag**, the US parent, and its CEO Len Hadley.
- **Central conflict and escalation:**
  1. The logic of the offer. Most people never redeem, and the travel agency would earn money on hotel and car add-ons.
  2. August 1992: European flights. November 1992: they add the *USA*.
  3. People buy vacuums only for the flights. Hoover expected about 50,000 applications and got about 300,000.
  4. The redemption forms get impossibly fiddly and the travel partner collapses.
  5. Customers fight back. The pressure group buys Maytag shares and confronts the CEO at the Iowa shareholder meeting in 1993, and BBC *Watchdog* investigates.
- **Twist:** The customers became shareholders to ambush the parent company in its own hometown. Meanwhile three top Hoover Europe executives were fired, Maytag set aside about $30M (1993) to charter planes (later estimates put the total at £48-50M or more), and Hoover Europe was sold to Candy in 1995.
- **Payoff:** Court cases ran until 1998. A "free" offer cost far more than the vacuums ever earned. The closing lesson: never build a promotion on the hope that customers are lazy.
- **Key verifiable figures/dates:**
  - Aug-Nov 1992.
  - £100 purchase for two flights worth about £600.
  - About 50k expected versus about 300k applicants.
  - Three executives fired (AP 1993).
  - Maytag's about $30M reserve (1993 report) and a total of about $50M per the Maytag Wikipedia entry.
  - Sale to Candy in 1995.
  - Litigation to 1998.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoover_free_flights_promotion (cites BBC 2004, Telegraph 2012, Des Moines Register 1993)
  - https://scholar.lib.vt.edu/VA-news/ROA-Times/issues/1993/rt9309/930930/09300102.htm (1993 contemporaneous report: $30M, executives)
  - https://www.marketingweek.com/free-flights-cases-mount-for-hoover/
  - https://www.chiefmarketer.com/a-bloody-dust-up/
  - https://thehustle.co/the-worst-sales-promotion-in-history
- **Disputed:**
  - The total cost varies: $30M reserve (1993), about £48-50M, and The Hustle's "£100m+" estimate. Present it as a range and name the source.
  - The famous anecdote about a customer seizing a Hoover van is UNVERIFIED in this run, so use it only if the fact-checker finds a solid source.
  - The Royal Warrant withdrawal is stated on Wikipedia, but its link to the fiasco needs checking.
- **Saturation:** MEDIUM. It is a staple of "worst marketing ever" articles, and some YouTube videos exist, but I found no large dedicated video in search. View counts are unknown, so confirm in Chrome. It is less known to the US audience, who are most of ours.
- **Money visual:** A seesaw with a £100 vacuum on one side and £600 of plane tickets on the other, crashing down. A counter labelled "applications" spins from 50,000 to 300,000 while a Hoover executive sweats.
- **~5-minute fit:** Excellent. There is a clear setup, decision, escalation, revolt and collapse, and it works well comedically with our character.

## 5. Two billionaire brothers tried to buy all the silver in the world
- **Pillar:** 5 (scheme, power, collapse)
- **Working title:** "Two Brothers Tried to Buy All the World's Silver. It Almost Worked"
- **Alt title:** "The Richest Family in America vs. the Price of Silver"
- **One-sentence premise:** In 1979 the Texan oil heirs Nelson Bunker and Herbert Hunt bought so much silver that the price went from about $9 to about $50, until the exchange changed the rules mid-game and the whole thing collapsed in a single day.
- **Opening-hook image:** People queue around the block to melt down their grandmother's silverware, while two cowboys sit on a mountain of silver bars.
- **Characters:**
  - **The Hunt brothers:** sons of oilman H.L. Hunt, who distrust paper money and fear inflation.
  - **Mahmoud Fustok:** a Saudi-linked partner, later found liable alongside them.
  - **COMEX and the regulators:** the "referees."
  - **Tiffany & Co.:** it buys a full-page ad calling the hoarding "unconscionable."
- **Central conflict and escalation:**
  1. Hunt-related holdings reach about 123M ounces by July 1979 and about 195M by January 1980.
  2. The price runs from about $9 (July 1979) to $34.45 (December 31, 1979) and then above $50 (January 1980).
  3. On January 7, 1980, COMEX caps positions. On January 21 it allows liquidation-only trading, so nobody can buy.
  4. The price slides. The Hunts are borrowing to buy and must post more cash as it falls (margin calls).
  5. Tiffany ad on March 26, 1980. On Silver Thursday, March 27, 1980, silver closes at $10.80.
- **Twist:** The billionaires do not go down alone. Their brokers and banks are exposed, so a 13-bank, $1.1B loan to the family firm Placid Oil props them up. Then in 1988 a jury finds them liable for conspiring to manipulate the market, and they file for bankruptcy the same year.
- **Payoff:** The family once called one of the richest in America ends up in bankruptcy court, and the "inflation-proof" metal did them in. The theme: you can't buy the whole game.
- **Key verifiable figures/dates:**
  - Holdings and prices above (SEC-based estimates).
  - COMEX rules of January 7 and January 21, 1980.
  - Tiffany ad in the NYT, March 26, 1980.
  - $10.80 close on March 27, 1980.
  - $1.1B Placid loan.
  - Minpeco verdict in August 1988, about $134M.
  - Chapter 11 in September 1988.
  - CFTC settlement in 1989 with a trading ban.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_Thursday
  - https://www.deseret.com/1988/8/21/18775716/3-hunt-guilty-in-plot-to-corner-silver-market (AP 1988 verdict)
  - https://leagle.com/decision/1989886718fsupp1681851 (Minpeco v. Hunt court decision)
  - https://www.bullionvault.com/gold-news/history/silver-hunt-rule-7-corner-010520261
  - https://csmonitor.com/1981/0528/052804.html (contemporaneous)
- **Disputed:**
  - Whether this was a deliberate "corner" or an inflation hedge. The Hunts always denied manipulation, but the civil jury found it. Say "a jury found."
  - Some argue the rule change by COMEX insiders (who were short silver) was self-interested. This is a real debate; present both sides.
  - The quote "a billion dollars isn't what it used to be" is UNVERIFIED, so do not use it.
  - The peak price varies by source ($48.70 to $50).
  - The single-day crash figures are often misstated. Use the $10.80 close, and cite the prior price only from a solid source.
- **Saturation:** MEDIUM-HIGH. It is a perennial finance topic with many bullion-dealer videos, podcasts and commentary ("History Meets Finance" and others). I could not find view counts. Finance-niche audiences know it; general audiences mostly don't.
- **Money visual:** A seesaw scale of "everyone else's silver" versus the Hunts' pile. A margin call shown as a loan shark tapping his watch. The rule change shown as the referee picking up the ball and walking off the pitch.
- **~5-minute fit:** Good. The arc is clean (rise, rule change, crash, trial) but needs one plain-language minute on "buying with borrowed money."

## 6. (Re-use of a Shorts subject) Blockbuster did NOT just laugh at Netflix; it nearly won
- **Pillar:** 5 (rivalry and a bad decision). We did this as a Short, and the long-form angle is the *verified, fuller* version.
- **Working title:** "Blockbuster Didn't Lose to Netflix. It Fired the Guy Who Was Winning"
- **Alt title:** "The $50 Million Mistake Everyone Tells Wrong"
- **One-sentence premise:** Everyone knows Blockbuster turned down Netflix for $50M. Almost nobody knows that, six years later, Blockbuster had Netflix on the ropes, and then a boardroom war threw it away.
- **Opening-hook image:** A tiny Netflix stickman in a giant Blockbuster office, holding a sign that says "$50M?" and drowning in the silence.
- **Characters:**
  - **Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph:** Netflix founders.
  - **Barry McCarthy:** Netflix CFO.
  - **John Antioco:** Blockbuster CEO.
  - **Carl Icahn:** activist investor.
  - **Jim Keyes:** Antioco's successor.
- **Central conflict and escalation:**
  1. 2000: Netflix is losing money (Hastings: on track to lose at least $50M that year) and pitches a sale for $50M. Blockbuster passes.
  2. Netflix grows.
  3. Antioco fights back. In 2004 he launches Blockbuster Online, in 2005 he drops late fees (about $140M in reported lost revenue in the first quarter), and in 2006 he launches *Total Access*, which lets you return mail DVDs in store for a free rental.
  4. Netflix feels it. Before Total Access, Netflix was getting about 70% of new subscribers (per Gina Keating's *Netflixed*). Hastings later said it "could have killed us."
  5. Icahn fights Antioco over strategy and pay. Antioco leaves in 2007, and Keyes raises Total Access prices and cuts it back.
- **Twist:** The famous decision (2000) is not what killed Blockbuster, or at least it is not the whole story. The less famous one is: abandoning the plan that was working. Even Icahn later praised Total Access and called Blockbuster "the worst investment I ever made."
- **Payoff:** Blockbuster files Chapter 11 in September 2010. The lesson is that a mistake becomes fatal when you undo the fix.
- **What is SOLID (verification note for Joe's question):**
  - A 2000 meeting took place in Dallas in which Netflix proposed being acquired for $50M and Blockbuster declined. This is confirmed independently by three Netflix insiders (Randolph's book *That Will Never Work*, 2019; Hastings's book *No Rules Rules*, 2020; McCarthy's interviews).
  - Blockbuster's own later strategy (Online 2004, Total Access 2006), Antioco's 2007 exit after the Icahn fight, and the 2010 bankruptcy are all documented.
  - Antioco and Icahn each wrote their side in HBR (April 2011).
- **What is DISPUTED or one-sided:**
  - "Antioco laughed / struggled not to laugh" comes only from Randolph's memoir, and Blockbuster never gave its side of the meeting.
  - The exact date (early 2000 versus September 2000) and who asked the price differ between Hastings and Randolph.
  - Whether $50M was the price every time Netflix pitched is unclear.
  - "Blockbuster would have 10M online subscribers" is Antioco's self-serving counterfactual.
  - The "Netflix lost 55k subscribers in 2007" figure is unsourced, so do not use it.
  - Many retellings say "Blockbuster laughed them out of the room" as fact. We should not.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.cnbc.com/2020/09/22/how-netflix-almost-lost-the-movie-rental-wars-to-blockbuster.html
  - https://marketplace.org/2020/09/08/ceo-reed-hastings-on-how-netflix-beat-blockbuster
  - https://sports.yahoo.com/blockbuster-ceo-struggled-not-laugh-191553438.html (Randolph excerpt)
  - https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20110318006089/en/Happened-Blockbuster-Story-John-Antioco-Carl-Icahn (HBR 2011 exchange)
  - https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/news/antioco-icahn-totally-total-access-135898
  - https://todayifoundout.com/?p=60247
- **Saturation:** VERY HIGH for "Blockbuster turned down Netflix," with many multi-million-view videos plus our own Short. The "nearly won / fired the winner" angle is less common but is used by some (CNBC's headline is literally "Netflix didn't kill Blockbuster").
- **Money visual:** A tug-of-war rope with a subscriber-share marker swinging from Netflix's side towards Blockbuster's (Total Access), then Icahn cuts the rope. The $50M price tag versus a later Netflix valuation tower (any valuation figure must be fact-checked at the date used).
- **~5-minute fit:** Good, with a strong three-act structure. The risk is that the audience thinks "I've seen this," so the title must lead with the reversal.
