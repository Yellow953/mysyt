# Story Hunt H2: Ridiculous plans that became real

Hunter H2, 2026-11-03. Seam: outrageous experiments, failed megaprojects, absurd inventions, bureaucratic madness, done on purpose by serious people.
Method: WebSearch/WebFetch for facts; YouTube saturation checked by reading YouTube search result pages (read-only, Claude in Chrome) on 2026-11-03. View counts are what the search page showed that day; only the first page was read, so a big video lower down could be missed.
Excluded per brief: Nauru, Onoda, Lustig, Fordlandia, Bone Wars, Shackleton, Koepcke, molasses flood, laughter epidemic, paperclip house, Emu War, Dyatlov, Mansa Musa, Pitcairn.

8 stories below, strongest first. Considered and dropped: Project Seal (NZ/US WWII "tsunami bomb", real and barely covered on YouTube, but no human arc and no ending: the tests just stop); Lake Chagan "atomic lake" (Soviet nuke made a lake; the minister-swimming detail could not be verified, and there is no story beyond the image); Starfish Prime and Camp Century/Iceworm (both real, but big science channels have covered them).

## 1. The Groundnut Army (Tanganyika Groundnut Scheme, 1947-1951)

- **Working title:** Britain Sent an Army of Tanks to Grow Peanuts
- **One-sentence pitch:** After WWII, Britain's government sent ex-soldiers and converted Sherman tanks into the African bush to grow peanuts on millions of acres. Four years and £36 million later, they had harvested less than the seed they had planted.
- **Opening-hook image:** A Sherman tank with a bulldozer blade where the gun should be, stuck against a giant baobab tree. Mr. Yellow Scarf in a pith helmet is holding a single peanut.
- **Central conflict / escalation:** Postwar Britain is short of cooking fat. A businessman (Frank Samuel of the United Africa Company) pitches peanuts in Tanganyika to Food Minister John Strachey. The survey takes only weeks and there is no pilot farm. The plan is about 3 million acres. About 100,000 ex-soldiers volunteer for roughly 1,200 jobs. Then everything goes wrong in order: two-thirds of the tractors are broken by the end of summer 1947, so they convert surplus Sherman tanks ("Shervicks") and those fail too. They drag a ship's anchor chain between bulldozers to flatten the bush. Elephants, rhinos and bees' nests inside baobabs put workers in hospital. A flash flood washes away the workshops. Then the dry season bakes the clay soil so hard the peanuts can't be dug up, and a drought follows. The target keeps shrinking: 3.2M acres, then 150,000, then 50,000. Only 47,000 acres are ever cleared.
- **Biggest twist / reveal:** After two years they harvest about 2,000 tons of groundnuts, less than they imported as seed. Also: managers in Tanganyika reportedly cancelled the order for the anchor chain because they thought it was a joke (Wikipedia marks this "citation needed").
- **Ending / payoff:** The scheme is cancelled in January 1951 and the Treasury writes off about £36.5M (over £1 billion today). John Gunther later called it "the worst fiasco in recent British colonial history." The cleared land ends up with local farmers growing tobacco and cashews. It worked for everyone except the planners.
- **Sources:**
  - Wikipedia, Tanganyika groundnut scheme (cites Westcott 2020, Wood 1950, Rizzo 2006, Gunther 1955): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanganyika_groundnut_scheme
  - Museum of English Rural Life (University of Reading), "Colonial failure" online exhibition: https://merl.reading.ac.uk/explore/online-exhibitions/colonial-failure/
  - History Today, "Britain Abandons the Groundnuts Scheme": https://www.historytoday.com/archive/britain-abandons-groundnuts-scheme
  - Matteo Rizzo (2006), Journal of Agrarian Change (SOAS eprint): https://eprints.soas.ac.uk/12959/1/Rizzo_P20.pdf
  - Primary footage: British Pathé "Groundnuts Scheme (1949)" and "Groundnuts - The Facts (1949)" are on YouTube.
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** Sources give different planned acreages (3M, 3.21M, 150k). The anchor-chain-cancelled-as-a-joke anecdote and the "Half London" nickname are marked citation needed on Wikipedia. A 1948 Spectator piece defended the work as "magnificent", so the "fiasco" label was argued about at the time. Avoid making Tanganyikans the punchline: the joke is on the London planners.
- **YouTube saturation:** LOW. Biggest result was a British Pathé newsreel at 11K. Next were Farming Explained at 7.4K, Vintage Scandals at 4.5K and roisin! at 1.3K, plus a scatter of Shorts under 2K. Nobody big has done it.
- **Needs more than ~5 min?** No. The story is a clean five-act fall and fits 5 minutes.

## 2. The Invasion of an Empty Island (Kiska, 1943)

- **Working title:** 34,000 Soldiers Invaded an Island. Nobody Was There.
- **One-sentence pitch:** In 1943, 34,000 American and Canadian troops stormed a Japanese-held island in Alaska after weeks of bombing. The enemy had left three weeks earlier, and the invasion still cost hundreds of casualties.
- **Opening-hook image:** Mr. Yellow Scarf in a helmet, crouched in thick fog and pointing his rifle at a confused stray dog wagging its tail.
- **Central conflict / escalation:** Japan occupied Kiska and Attu in 1942, the only US soil occupied in WWII. After the bloody battle for Attu, the Allies prepare a huge landing on Kiska. On 26 July 1943, US battleships fire 518 large shells at radar contacts in the fog (the "Battle of the Pips") and hit nothing. The contacts were probably birds or radar echoes. While the fleet pulls back to refuel, Japanese ships slip in under fog on 28 July and take the whole garrison off in under an hour. The Allies keep bombing the empty island for about two more weeks. On 15 August they land 34,000+ men.
- **Biggest twist / reveal:** No one is there except a few dogs. Then the nervous troops, in fog, shoot at each other. Friendly fire, booby traps and mines cause the casualties. The destroyer USS Abner Read hits a mine and 71 men die.
- **Ending / payoff:** The largest invasion of the Alaska campaign "captures" an empty island at a cost of roughly 300+ casualties (counts vary). Robert Sherrod's report on the friendly fire was censored at the time. It's the ultimate worst deal: winning a battle against no one and still losing.
- **Sources:**
  - Wikipedia, Operation Cottage: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Cottage
  - Wikipedia, Battle of the Pips (518 shells, 26-27 July 1943): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Pips
  - US Naval Institute Proceedings, McCandless, "The Battle of the Pips" (Feb 1958): https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1958/february/battle-pips
  - Task & Purpose, "WWII battle on an empty island left 300 dead and wounded": https://taskandpurpose.com/history/wwii-battle-empty-island-left-300-dead-wounded/
  - Project 44 (Canadian), Kiska 15 August 1943: https://project44.ca/kiska/
  - National Park Service, Kiska NHL document: https://www.nps.gov/subjects/nhlalaska/upload/Japanese-Occupation-Site-National-Historic-Landmark-Kiska-Island_508.pdf
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** Casualty totals range from about 313 to "over 500" depending on whether the Abner Read, accidents and illness are counted. The most cited friendly-fire figure is 28 US and 4 Canadians killed, but verify it before scripting. What the "pips" were (birds or terrain echoes) is unknown. The dogs are widely reported but I did not check them against a primary record. Treat the "dogs only" line as supported, not verified.
- **YouTube saturation:** MEDIUM-LOW but crowded. Search shows about 15 small long-form uploads in the last 3 months (mostly under 100 views, several look AI-made; the largest is BrinkLore at 1.4K) and Shorts up to 11K. No big channel owns it. Lots of creators have found the topic, but nobody has made a winner. The Battle of the Pips half is barely used.
- **Needs more than ~5 min?** No. The best cut is Pips, then escape, then landing, then friendly fire.

## 3. A Crank's Hole at the North Pole Founded the Smithsonian (Symmes, Reynolds and the Wilkes Expedition)

- **Working title:** One Man Swore the Earth Was Hollow. Congress Listened.
- **One-sentence pitch:** In 1818 an American army captain mailed a "certificate of sanity" with his claim that the Earth is hollow with holes at the poles. His campaign dragged Congress into funding a huge naval expedition, which mapped Antarctica and filled what became the Smithsonian.
- **Opening-hook image:** Mr. Yellow Scarf peering down into a giant hole at the North Pole, holding a lantern, with a signed "CERTIFICATE OF SANITY" pinned to his coat.
- **Central conflict / escalation:** John Cleves Symmes Jr., a War of 1812 veteran, mails a circular (10 April 1818) to colleges and officials announcing "the earth is hollow and habitable within." He asks for 100 brave companions to sail to the polar opening. Nobody signs up, so he lectures for years and petitions Congress, which votes on it twice. A newspaper editor disciple, Jeremiah Reynolds, takes over, quietly drops the hollow-earth part and lobbies for a South Pole voyage. President John Quincy Adams backs it, then loses the 1828 election and the plan dies. Reynolds goes privately in 1829, and his crew mutinies in Chile and leaves him there for about two years. He comes back, addresses the House in 1836, and Congress finally funds it, with Adams (now a congressman) casting a deciding vote.
- **Biggest twist / reveal:** The expedition born from a hollow-earth crank becomes the US Exploring Expedition (Wilkes, 1838-42), six ships that help establish Antarctica as a continent. And Reynolds, who made it happen, is not invited.
- **Ending / payoff:** The expedition's roughly 40 tons of specimens become the core of the US National Museum, which is the Smithsonian's natural history collection. A man who wanted to find the people living inside the Earth accidentally helped found America's national museum.
- **Sources:**
  - History.com, Dave Roos, "Expedition to the Center of the Earth": https://www.history.com/articles/expedition-to-the-center-of-the-earth
  - Massachusetts Historical Society blog, "Journey to the South Pole with John Quincy Adams" (Adams diaries): https://masshist.org/beehiveblog/2024/03/journey-to-the-south-pole-with-john-quincy-adams-charles-francis-adams-part-2
  - Smithsonian Institution Archives, US Exploring Expedition (collections formed the core of the US National Museum): https://siarchives.si.edu/collections/auth_exp_fbr_eace0051
  - Smithsonian Magazine on the expedition: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/remembering-the-last-great-worldwide-sailing-expedition-4763810/
  - Book: Nathaniel Philbrick, *Sea of Glory* (2003).
  - SF Encyclopedia entry on Symmes: https://sf-encyclopedia.com/entry/symmes_john_cleves
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** The vote counts on Symmes's petitions differ between sources ("25 yays" vs 56-46 vs tabled). It is a simplification to say Symmes caused the Wilkes Expedition: Reynolds explicitly dropped hollow-earth theory, and the expedition had commercial and naval motives (whaling, sealing, charting). Script it as "the campaign began with Symmes," not "Congress funded a hollow-earth trip." The well-known legend that Poe called out "Reynolds!" on his deathbed is unverified, so skip it or flag it as legend. "Proved Antarctica is a continent" is popular phrasing; the safer line is "charted 1,500 miles of Antarctic coast and argued it was a continent."
- **YouTube saturation:** MEDIUM for hollow earth in general (Extra History Short 2.9M, Ninjas Are Butterflies 1.1M, History channel 1.2M), LOW for this particular chain. Symmes-specific long-form videos are small (Bad Ideas 1.4K, Michael Kent 1.6K, GrampawUniverse 35K). I saw no video built around "hollow-earth crank, then Antarctica, then Smithsonian."
- **Needs more than ~5 min?** Borderline. It has three protagonists (Symmes, Reynolds, Wilkes). It fits 5 min only if Wilkes's own drama (court-martial etc.) is cut.

## 4. Lake-Cow Bacon (the American Hippo Bill, 1910)

- **Working title:** Congress Almost Put Hippos in Louisiana (Pitched by Two Men Who'd Tried to Kill Each Other)
- **One-sentence pitch:** In 1910 a US congressman proposed importing hippos into Louisiana's swamps to fix a meat shortage and an invasive-plant crisis at once. His star witnesses were two adventurers who had been under orders to kill each other in the Boer War, and one of them later ran the biggest Nazi spy ring ever caught in America.
- **Opening-hook image:** Mr. Yellow Scarf at a Louisiana BBQ in a cowboy hat, flipping a giant strip of "LAKE COW BACON" while a hippo in the bayou stares at him.
- **Central conflict / escalation:** America has a meat shortage, and water hyacinth is choking the bayous. Rep. Robert Broussard's bill H.R. 23261 asks for $250,000 to import hippos, which would eat the weeds and then be eaten. Experts testify, including Frederick Russell Burnham (famous scout) and Fritz Duquesne (Boer adventurer, con man). Theodore Roosevelt, the USDA, the Washington Post and the New York Times ("lake cow bacon") reportedly support it. A USDA man claims it could add a million tons of meat a year.
- **Biggest twist / reveal:** Burnham and Duquesne were enemies. In the Boer War, Burnham was reportedly ordered to kill Duquesne and vice versa. Now they share a stage selling hippos. Duquesne goes on to lead the Duquesne Spy Ring, 33 German agents arrested by the FBI in 1941.
- **Ending / payoff:** The bill dies in committee (often said to be one vote short). No hippos come to America. The callback: Pablo Escobar's escaped hippos are now multiplying in Colombia, so we got to see the alternate ending anyway.
- **Sources:**
  - Wikipedia, American Hippo bill: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Hippo_bill
  - Salon (2022), "The true story of when Congress almost released wild hippos into the Louisiana bayou": https://salon.com/2022/09/04/the-true-story-of-when-congress-almost-released-wild-hippos-into-the-louisiana-bayou
  - Gizmodo, "The remarkable early 20th-century plan to farm hippopotamuses": https://gizmodo.com/the-remarkable-early-20th-century-plan-to-farm-hippopot-1493356004
  - Wikipedia, Fritz Duquesne (Boer War, 1941 spy ring): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Duquesne
  - Book: Jon Mooallem, *American Hippopotamus* (The Atavist, 2013), the main narrative source; I could not open it here.
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** "Failed by one vote" is repeated widely but I could not find it in a primary record, so mark it as "reportedly". Roosevelt's support is reported second-hand. Duquesne's Boer War exploits are largely self-mythologised (one source says hard facts are "few and far between"), and the mutual kill-orders are "reported" rather than documented. The 1941 spy ring is solid (FBI history). Bill number: H.R. 23261 (one source says 23621).
- **YouTube saturation:** MEDIUM-HIGH. Today I Found Out 348K, Oddly Informative 208K, plus many small Shorts. The Duquesne enemies-turned-partners angle is underused, but the core premise has been done well.
- **Needs more than ~5 min?** No.

## 5. The Rats Without Tails (Great Hanoi Rat Massacre, 1902)

- **Working title:** Hanoi Paid People to Kill Rats. They Got More Rats.
- **One-sentence pitch:** To fight plague, French colonial Hanoi paid one cent per rat tail. People started cutting tails off live rats and releasing them to breed, smuggling rats in from the countryside, and finally farming rats.
- **Opening-hook image:** A happy, tail-less rat strolling down a sewer, past Mr. Yellow Scarf in a colonial official's uniform who is weeping over a ledger.
- **Central conflict / escalation:** French Hanoi builds a modern sewer system for the European quarter, which turns out to be a rat superhighway. Plague is spreading worldwide. Officials hire Vietnamese rat-catchers, and kill counts climb into the thousands per day (about 20,000 on 21 June 1902). That isn't enough, so they open a public bounty: one cent per tail.
- **Biggest twist / reveal:** Officials notice tail-less rats running around. Hunters were cutting off tails and releasing the rats to breed more income. Then people smuggled rats in from outside the city, and then rat farms appeared on the outskirts.
- **Ending / payoff:** The bounty is scrapped, and plague reaches Hanoi anyway (reported cases in 1903). It became the textbook "cobra effect / Goodhart's law" case. Payoff: "When you pay for tails, you get tails."
- **Sources:**
  - Michael G. Vann (historian), IIAS The Review, "Rats and Sewers in 'Modern' and 'Civilized' French Hanoi": https://www.iias.asia/the-review/rats-and-sewers-modern-and-civilized-french-hanoi
  - Atlas Obscura, Hanoi rat massacre 1902: https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/hanoi-rat-massacre-1902
  - Wikipedia, Great Hanoi Rat Massacre: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Hanoi_Rat_Massacre
  - Book: Michael G. Vann and Liz Clarke, *The Great Hanoi Rat Hunt* (Oxford UP, 2019, graphic history).
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** Day-by-day numbers come from Vann's archival work and haven't been independently re-checked. The rat-farm detail is reported, but its scale is unknown. It is often merged with the apocryphal "Delhi cobra bounty"; don't do that.
- **YouTube saturation:** MEDIUM-LOW as a standalone story. Dedicated videos are small (This Dark History 1K, Storyin 694, Shorts up to 7K), but it is a staple example inside big "cobra effect" and "perverse incentive" videos, so many viewers know the punchline.
- **Needs more than ~5 min?** No. If anything it's thin for 5 minutes. It needs the sewer and plague setup to carry the first half.

## 6. The Price of a Country (Darien Scheme, 1698-1700)

- **Working title:** Scotland Bet Its Whole Country on a Swamp in Panama
- **One-sentence pitch:** In the 1690s Scotland poured a huge share of its money into a colony in the Panama jungle. About 2,000 people died, and the bankrupt nation was compensated as part of the deal that ended its independence.
- **Opening-hook image:** Mr. Yellow Scarf in a kilt, standing in a steaming jungle with a trunk full of wool and wigs to sell, while a parrot laughs at him.
- **Central conflict / escalation:** Scotland is poor and shut out of English trade. William Paterson (co-founder of the Bank of England) pitches a colony at Darien that would make Scotland the hub between the Atlantic and Pacific. The public subscribes en masse: estimates run from about 20% of the money circulating to a quarter or half of the country's liquid wealth. The ships sail with trade goods ill-suited to the tropics (the wool and wigs are a popular claim; verify). They arrive in November 1698 and found "New Caledonia." Fever, no trade (England forbids its colonies to help), Spanish attack. A second expedition arrives to find the first colony abandoned.
- **Biggest twist / reveal:** Under the 1707 Treaty of Union, England paid Scotland £398,085 (the "Equivalent"), partly to compensate Darien investors. Critics called it the price of Scotland.
- **Ending / payoff:** A jungle trading post is part of why Great Britain exists. Worst deal in history at national scale.
- **Sources:**
  - National Library of Scotland, "Scotland and Darien": https://nls.uk/exhibitions/scotland-and-darien
  - Wikipedia, Darien scheme: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darien_scheme
  - History Files, New Caledonia: https://www.historyfiles.co.uk/KingListsAmericas/CentralNewCaledonia.htm
  - Book: John Prebble, *The Darien Disaster* (1968); Douglas Watt, *The Price of Scotland* (2007).
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** The share of national wealth lost varies widely (20% to 50%). How far Darien "caused" the Union is a real historians' debate; it is one factor, not the cause. The "they brought wigs and combs to the jungle" detail is popular but needs a primary check before use.
- **YouTube saturation:** HIGH-ish. Geographics 619K, The History Guy 87K, LoganProductions 18K, Econ Empires 8.6K and many Shorts. This is a known "weird history" staple in the UK.
- **Needs more than ~5 min?** Leans yes: two expeditions, politics and the Union. It can be squeezed into 5 min by cutting the second expedition to one beat.

## 7. The Hedge That Cut India in Half (Inland Customs Line, c.1840s-1879)

- **Working title:** The British Built a 1,500-Mile Hedge to Tax Salt
- **One-sentence pitch:** To collect a salt tax, the British in India grew a living hedge of thorny bushes across the subcontinent, guarded by about 12,000 men. Then it was forgotten so completely that the man who rediscovered it first thought it was a hoax.
- **Opening-hook image:** An endless wall of thorns stretching to the horizon. Mr. Yellow Scarf on one side holding a tiny bag of salt, a customs guard on the other.
- **Central conflict / escalation:** The East India Company taxes salt heavily, and smugglers walk it across internal borders. Customs officers start with a line of dead thorn fences, then plant a living hedge (Indian plum, prickly pear, babul), patrolled by thousands. At its peak the customs line ran about 2,300-2,500 miles, with the hedge itself about 1,100+ miles. Salt became so expensive that ordinary people could barely afford the amount they needed.
- **Biggest twist / reveal:** It was abandoned in 1879, not because the tax ended but because Britain now controlled salt production everywhere. It vanished from memory, until Roy Moxham found a footnote in a memoir in a London second-hand bookshop, thought it was a hoax, and spent years in India looking for it. He found only a small embankment in Etawah district.
- **Ending / payoff:** Callback: the salt tax it protected is the same one Gandhi marched against in 1930.
- **Sources:**
  - Roy Moxham, *The Great Hedge of India* (Constable/Little, Brown, 2001): https://www.littlebrown.co.uk/titles/roy-moxham/the-great-hedge-of-india/9781841194677
  - Atlas Obscura, "The British once built a 1,100-mile hedge through the middle of India": https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/colonial-india-british-hedge-salt-tax
  - Kirkus review of Moxham: https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/roy-moxham/the-great-hedge-of-india/
  - Live History India: https://www.livehistoryindia.com/story/snapshort-histories/the-great-hedge-of-india/
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** Lengths vary (the hedge about 1,100-1,500 miles; the customs line about 2,300-2,500 miles), so don't say "a 2,500-mile hedge." The causal links to famine deaths are argued by Moxham and others but are interpretive. "12,000 men" is a commonly cited peak staffing figure; verify against Moxham before use.
- **YouTube saturation:** HIGH. Thoughty2 601K (11 months ago), Alchemist Talks 373K, EmperorTigerstar 348K, Horrible Histories 22K, and many Shorts, one at 27K. A recent big-channel hit means low novelty.
- **Needs more than ~5 min?** No.

## 8. The Plan to Nuke a Harbor into Alaska (Project Chariot, 1958-1962)

- **Working title:** The US Wanted to Dig a Harbor with Hydrogen Bombs
- **One-sentence pitch:** In 1958 Edward Teller's lab planned to blast a harbor into the Arctic coast of Alaska with nuclear bombs, a harbor nobody needed and that would be iced over most of the year. When it was cancelled, the government secretly buried radioactive soil on the site, and locals only found out 30 years later.
- **Opening-hook image:** Mr. Yellow Scarf holding a shovel in the snow, next to a "Harbor Construction" sign and a bomb labeled "SHOVEL 2.0."
- **Central conflict / escalation:** It's part of Operation Plowshare, "peaceful" nukes. The plan calls for several thermonuclear devices (sources describe about 160 Hiroshimas in total) at Cape Thompson, about 30 miles from the Iñupiat village of Point Hope. Teller tours Alaska selling it. Government-funded biologists study the site, find the fallout would enter the lichen-caribou-human food chain, and say so; their careers suffer. Point Hope residents organise against it.
- **Biggest twist / reveal:** It's cancelled in 1962, but in August 1962 the USGS brought soil contaminated at the Nevada Test Site to Chariot for tracer experiments, then buried it in a mound and left. Point Hope residents learned of it about 30 years later (1992, through researcher Dan O'Neill's archive work). DOE removed about 162 cubic yards of soil in 1993.
- **Ending / payoff:** The fight helped politicise Alaska Native land claims, which fed into the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971. The bomb never went off, but the mound did happen.
- **Sources:**
  - US DOE Office of Legacy Management, Chariot, Alaska Site Fact Sheet (tracer test August 1962, Nevada soil, 1992 re-examination, 1993 removal of about 162 cubic yards): https://www.energy.gov/lm/articles/chariot-alaska-site-fact-sheet
  - Wikipedia, Project Chariot: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Chariot
  - Atlas Obscura, "The actual 1950s proposal to nuke Alaska": https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/actual-1950s-proposal-nuke-alaska
  - Anchorage Daily News (2014), residents' lingering suspicion: https://www.adn.com/rural-alaska/article/are-nuclear-bombs-buried-near-point-hope-alaska-some-believe-so/2014/03/03/
  - Book: Dan O'Neill, *The Firecracker Boys* (1994).
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** No nuclear device was ever detonated there; never imply it was. The "160 Hiroshimas" figure is the total yield of the original plan, which changed over time. Claims that bombs are still buried there are local suspicion, not established. That O'Neill personally surfaced the mound in 1992 comes from his book and secondary accounts; the DOE sheet confirms the 1992 re-examination but not who triggered it. The ANCSA link is commonly asserted; phrase it as "helped spark".
- **YouTube saturation:** HIGH. Joe Scott 418K (3 months ago), RealLifeLore 418K, Sideprojects 80K, plus Shorts up to 10K. It's well covered, though the buried-soil twist is less used.
- **Needs more than ~5 min?** No.

## Quick ranking note for the producer (not a score)
Freshest and strongest fit for the mascot: **Groundnut Army** (nearly untouched, comic escalation, clean ending), **Kiska** (perfect "worst deal" irony, no big competitor), **Symmes to Smithsonian** (great "wait, WHAT" chain, under-told as a single arc). Hippo and Hanoi are good but partly known. Darien, Great Hedge and Chariot are solid stories with big existing videos.
