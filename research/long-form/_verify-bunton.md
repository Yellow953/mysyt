## Verdict: USABLE WITH CAVEATS

(a) Happened as pitched: The theft, the £140,000 / TV licence demand, the return by left-luggage, the July 1965 surrender and the November 1965 trial outcome (acquitted of the painting, convicted of the frame, 3 months) are VERIFIED, mostly via the National Gallery's own archive record. The pitched twist, "his son actually took it and the file was sealed until 2012", is only partly established.

(b) Beats in order: 21 Aug 1961 theft (19 days after it went on display, just after being saved for the nation) -> ransom notes (Reuters, Exchange Telegraph) for £140,000 to pay pensioners' TV licences -> Scotland Yard looks for a master thief, Dr. No (1962) puts the painting in a villain's lair -> May 1965 painting returned via left-luggage in Birmingham -> 19 July 1965 Bunton surrenders -> Nov 1965 Old Bailey: not guilty of the painting, guilty of the frame, 3 months -> law later changes -> 1969 John tells police he did it -> file sealed, released 2012.

(c) Disputed / legend / careful wording:
1. Who actually took the painting: Kempton's own confession and trial vs John's 1969 statement. Neither is proven in court. Wording: "Bunton always said he did it alone. In 1969 his son John told police he was the one who took it. Nobody was ever prosecuted."
2. "Covering for his son": speculation, no source.
3. Job (bus driver vs taxi driver) differs by source.
4. "Government bought it for £140,000": wrong; Wolfson £100k + Treasury £40k.
5. Theft Act causation: plausible, secondary sources only.
6. "17 stone", "jailed several times for TV licence", "five notes" (one missing from the Gallery's records): single-source or self-reported.
7. The Dr. No tie: it's a coincidence of filming (1962 premiere, painting in the lair); no claim he got the idea from it.
8. Film vs reality: Dorothy's role largely invented in film.

(d) Enough material for 5 min with surprises intact? Yes, structurally. The surprises: "kidnapped" painting held for pensioners' TV licences; four years of silence; he walked in on his own; acquitted of the painting, convicted of the frame; the film's prop; and in 1969 a son's confession in a sealed file. But the twist-ending depends on how it is worded, and the strongest single-claim version ("his son did it, he took the fall") is not proven.

(e) Shaky claims the appeal depends on: "The son did it" as fact; "went to prison to protect him"; the "TV licence" motive is solid but his surrender motive is unknown. Competition: a 132K Lume video (3 months old) already covers the son twist (hunt, not verified by me), plus the 2022 film.

Research limitations: Guardian (Travis, 30 Nov 2012), National Archives DPP file and blog, History Today, Scotsman, Londonist and Hirsch's book could not be opened (403/405/blocked), so the 2012 release and John's confession details rest on NGA26 (which states the 1969 confession), Wikipedia citations and search summaries. No trial transcript read. Wikipedia used only as a lead. The Gallery's own archive record and its 2021 archive release (via reposts of the National Gallery text) are the strongest sources I opened.
