## 1. The Man Who Invented Paper Money Was Sentenced to Death
- **Pillar:** 2 (money). Could also sit in pillar 5.
- **Working titles:** "He Invented Paper Money. They Sentenced Him to Death." / "Europe's First Banknote Got Its Inventor Sentenced to Death"
- **Premise (one sentence):** In 1660s Sweden, money was copper slabs you could barely lift, so a banker printed paper to replace them, printed too much, and ended up condemned to die.
- **Opening hook image:** Mr. Yellow Scarf on a market street, bent double under a huge copper plate strapped to his back like a fridge, trying to buy a loaf of bread. The baker hands back change: another copper slab.
- **Central conflict and escalation:** Johan Palmstruch gets a royal charter for Stockholms Banco (1656/57). In 1660 the crown mints new, lighter coins; depositors rush to get their heavier old coins back (a bank run). His fix: paper "credit notes" (1661) that anyone can trade instead of carrying metal. It works so well that he hands out notes as loans, far beyond the metal in the vault. More notes, lower value (an early inflation). Confidence cracks, everyone shows up at once to swap paper for copper, and the vault is short.
- **Twist:** 1664: the state orders the notes withdrawn. 1668: Palmstruch is sentenced to death for mismanaging the bank, while the state takes over his bank, and that bank becomes the Riksbank, the oldest central bank still operating. The man is punished; his idea becomes the system.
- **Payoff:** Reprieved, jailed until 1670, dead in 1671. Every banknote in your wallet runs on his exact bet (people trusting paper they can't eat), and on his exact failure mode (print too much and trust breaks). Closing line idea: "The paper survived. The man didn't get the credit. Fittingly, he also didn't get the copper."
- **Sources:**
  - Sveriges Riksbank, "1661 - First banknotes in Europe": https://www.riksbank.se/en-gb/about-the-riksbank/history/historical-timeline/1600-1699/first-banknotes-in-europe/
  - New York Fed, Liberty Street Economics, "Historical Echoes: Central Bank and Paper Money Innovator Given Death Sentence...": https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2013/04/historical-echoes-central-bank-and-paper-money-innovator-given-death-sentence-for-his-efforts
  - BIS speech (Riksbank governor) citing the 22 July 1668 sentence: https://www.bis.org/review/r170426d.pdf
  - Riksbank 350-year history PDF: https://www.riksbank.se/globalassets/media/riksbanken-350-ar/digital-skrift/sveriges-riksbank-a-350-year-jurney.pdf
  - History Today: https://www.historytoday.com/archive/europe%E2%80%99s-first-banknotes-issued-sweden
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** (a) The death sentence: Riksbank and BIS say death, commuted; Flux's 1905 history mentions only life imprisonment (NY Fed notes the gap). Trial records were not published. Say "sentenced to death, later spared" and note sources differ, or say "condemned". (b) How much was fraud vs bad lending is unknown; associates may have escaped blame (NY Fed). Do not call him a crook. (c) Copper plate weight: the plate-money angle is real (Riksbank calls the coins "heavy and cumbersome"), but the often-quoted "up to ~20 kg" figure is UNVERIFIED by me; fact-check before using a number. (d) "First paper money" must be "first banknotes in Europe" (China had paper money centuries earlier). Say so on screen; it actually helps the line.
- **Saturation:** Written coverage is solid (Business Insider, History Today, central-bank blogs). I found no YouTube video on it in search results; could not see view counts. Likely low saturation on YouTube as a story video.
- **5-minute fit:** Strong. Clear protagonist, rising problem (heavy money, bank run, over-printing, crash), a verdict, and a modern payoff. Visual comedy is built in (copper slabs).

## 2. Nobody Wanted the Shopping Cart, So He Paid People to Pretend
- **Pillar:** 2 (shopping carts).
- **Working titles:** "Nobody Would Use the Shopping Cart. So He Hired Fake Shoppers." / "The Shopping Cart Flopped. Then He Faked It."
- **Premise:** A 1930s Oklahoma grocer invents the shopping cart and shoppers flatly refuse to touch it, so he hires people to push empty carts around his store until everyone else gives in.
- **Opening hook image:** A 1937 supermarket. Mr. Yellow Scarf, in a grocer's apron, stands proudly beside a gleaming new cart. Shoppers walk past it, arms full of baskets, eyes averted. One woman pats it like a stroller.
- **Central conflict and escalation:** Sylvan Goldman (Humpty Dumpty stores) notices customers stop shopping when their hand basket is full. Inspired by a folding chair, he and mechanic Fred Young build a folding frame with two baskets on wheels (introduced June 1937). It flops. He hires male and female models to push the carts and greeters to explain them. It catches on, he patents the folding design (1940), and he gets rich licensing it.
- **Twist:** Ten years later, Orla Watson invents the nesting cart (the one that slides into the next, the one we still use). Goldman's company makes a "Nest-Kart"; Watson's company pursues him for infringement. The settlement: Goldman gives up his patent rights and pays $1 in damages, in exchange for an exclusive license to make Watson's design. The "inventor of the shopping cart" ends up licensing someone else's cart.
- **Payoff:** The thing nobody wanted is now in every store on Earth, and the store makes more money because of it (bigger baskets, bigger shops). Goldman becomes a multimillionaire anyway. Moral beat: the hard part wasn't inventing it; it was getting a stranger to look silly first.
- **Sources:**
  - Wikipedia "Sylvan Goldman" (cites Terry P. Wilson, *The Cart that Changed the World*, 1978; Smithsonian NMAH Telescope cart collection; NPR 2025): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylvan_Goldman
  - Smithsonian Magazine (could not fetch, 403; title confirms the story): https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/industrious-grocer-1930s-wanted-make-easier-customers-buy-more-just-needed-push-180987633/
  - Oklahoma Hall of Fame: https://oklahomahof.com/hof/inductees/goldman-sylvan-n-1971
  - KGOU (NPR Oklahoma), "Was the shopping cart invented in Oklahoma?": https://www.kgou.org/show/how-curious/2024-11-26/was-the-shopping-cart-invented-in-oklahoma
  - CBS, Charles Kuralt 1977 interview with Goldman: https://cbsnews.com/videos/from-1977-charles-kuralt-on-shopping-carts
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** (a) Why people refused. "Men felt it was unmanly, women said it looked like a baby carriage" comes from weaker sources and Goldman's own retelling; label it "the story Goldman told". (b) Dates: 1936 vs 1937, patent filing 1937 vs grant 1940. (c) The "hired models" part is well attested; "attractive models" is a popular embellishment. (d) Other people had wheeled baskets earlier; Goldman is the first commercially successful one, not the only inventor.
- **Saturation:** MEDIUM-HIGH as a fact ("fun fact" articles, Fox News video, NPR, many blogs). I could not see YouTube view counts. The $1 settlement and Watson twist are much less told, so that's the fresh angle.
- **5-minute fit:** Good, but the first half is well known. Needs the Watson twist and the "fake shoppers" scene carried hard to fill 5 minutes without padding.

## 3. The Woman Who Sold the Time (and the Rival Who Tried to Ruin Her)
- **Pillar:** 2 (clocks). Leans toward pillar 4 (unbelievable real story).
- **Working titles:** "For 104 Years, a London Family Sold the Time" / "She Sold the Time Door to Door. Then a Company Tried to Destroy Her."
- **Premise:** Before radio time signals, a London woman carried the exact time to customers in her handbag, and a tech company tried to kill her business with a smear in *The Times*.
- **Opening hook image:** Mr. Yellow Scarf as a Victorian shopkeeper staring at three clocks on his wall, each showing a different time. A knock at the door: a small elderly woman holds up a pocket watch: "It's 9:04 and 12 seconds. That'll be four pounds a year."
- **Central conflict and escalation:** 1836: astronomer's assistant John Belville starts setting a fine chronometer at Greenwich each morning and visiting clients to sell them the correct time. His widow Maria takes over (1856), then daughter Ruth (1892). The watch, a 1794 John Arnold chronometer she calls "Arnold", was made for the Duke of Sussex. Rival tech arrives: the Standard Time Company sells telegraph time signals. 1908: its director St John Wynne publicly mocks her as out of date and insinuates she uses her femininity to win business; *The Times* prints the speech without saying he's her competitor. Reporters swarm her door.
- **Twist:** The smear backfires: the publicity brings her customers, and she calls it "free advertising". She keeps going for 32 more years, through the BBC pips (1924) and the speaking clock (1936).
- **Payoff:** She retires at about 85 in 1940 (partly the wartime danger of crossing London), dies in 1943; "Arnold" goes to the Clockmakers' Company, where it still is. Theme: a person beating a machine company, for a while.
- **Sources:**
  - Royal Museums Greenwich, "The Greenwich Time Lady": https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/greenwich-time-lady-ruth-belville
  - Linda Hall Library, Scientist of the Day: https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/ruth-belville/
  - Wikipedia "Ruth Belville" (cites David Rooney & Belville, *Ruth Belville: The Greenwich Time Lady*, 2008; Univ. of Houston "Engines of Our Ingenuity" No. 2070): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruth_Belville
  - Gizmodo, "The woman who kept time": https://gizmodo.com/the-woman-who-kept-time-5852886
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** (a) Customers: RMG says 30-40 weekly clients for Ruth; the "200 clients" figure is for her father's era (Wikipedia). (b) Prices: ~£4/year vs Post Office £15 (via Rooney); verify in Rooney before using numbers. (c) "104 years" is 1836-1940 for the family business, not Ruth alone. (d) The exact wording of Wynne's "femininity" insinuation comes through secondary sources; present it as implied, not as a quote. (e) John Belville's job title varies by source.
- **Saturation:** MEDIUM. Popular in "weird history" articles, QI, a BBC piece, podcasts (PopSci, London Walks). I found no YouTube video in the results; could not see views. The rival/smear angle as the spine is less common.
- **5-minute fit:** Good. Gentle conflict, though: the villain only gives one speech. The architect would need to build tension from the technology closing in (telegraph, BBC pips, speaking clock), not just Wynne.

## 4. The Son of "Praise-God Barebone" Invented Insurance After London Burned
- **Pillar:** 2 (insurance). Fire insurance, fire marks and private fire brigades.
- **Working titles:** "Insurance Was Invented by a Man With the Worst Name in History" / "After London Burned, One Man Started Selling Fire Insurance (With His Own Firefighters)"
- **Premise:** After the Great Fire of 1666 wiped out London, a property developer with a ridiculous Puritan name started selling fire insurance, then hired his own firefighters so he wouldn't have to pay out.
- **Opening hook image:** London 1666, the skyline burning; Mr. Yellow Scarf on a rooftop holding a bucket the size of a teacup. Smash cut to the morning after: a man in a wig walks through the ashes with a clipboard, smiling.
- **Central conflict and escalation:** Nicholas Barbon, a doctor turned speculative builder, is the son of Cromwell-era MP Praise-God Barebone. He begins insuring houses around 1667; in 1680 the Fire Office opens. Incentive problem: every fire costs him money, so the company runs its own brigade (Thames watermen in bright uniforms) and nails metal "fire marks" on insured houses. Rival insurers copy him; London ends up with competing private brigades.
- **Twist (myth-busting, best used deliberately):** The famous story that insurance brigades let uninsured houses burn is mostly myth: the London Fire Brigade Museum says "there is little real evidence" for it; brigades fought all fires, because fire spreads to your customers too. Real twist: the profit motive accidentally created public firefighting. In 1833 ten insurance brigades merged into the London Fire Engine Establishment, which became the city's Metropolitan Fire Brigade in the 1860s.
- **Payoff:** The fire marks are still on London buildings today. Your insurance and the fire service both come from one man trying not to pay claims.
- **Sources:**
  - London Fire Brigade Museum, "Early insurance brigades": https://www.london-fire.gov.uk/museum/london-fire-brigade-history-and-stories/early-insurance-brigades
  - Cabinet Magazine (Connelly), Barbon and his name: https://cabinetmagazine.org/issues/32/connelly.php
  - Insurance Hall of Fame, Nicholas Barbon: https://insurancehalloffame.org/node/10
  - Insurance Museum, "The father of fire insurance": https://insurance.museum/?p=5191
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** (a) His full baptismal name, "If-Jesus-Christ-had-not-died-for-thee-thou-hadst-been-damned" (wording varies; Cabinet gives "Unless-Christ-had-died..."), is a well-known anecdote but I could not confirm it from a primary record. UNVERIFIED: use only as "legend says his full name was..." The father's name, Praise-God Barebone, is real. (b) Start date 1667 vs 1680 (informal vs formal company). (c) "Let uninsured houses burn" is a myth; use it only as the debunk. (d) The date the public Metropolitan Fire Brigade began is unclear in what I read: my summary of the LFB page gave 1862, but the usual date is the 1865 Act (in force 1866). The fact-checker must confirm it before it's used.
- **Saturation:** LOW-MEDIUM. Insurance-industry blogs and "Great Fire invented insurance" listicles; I found no YouTube video in results.
- **5-minute fit:** Medium. Barbon himself is colourful (later economist, debts, rogue developer: UNVERIFIED by me, needs research), but the story goes from one person to an institution partway through. Works if Barbon stays the spine.

## 5. The Weekend Exists Because Workers Kept Skipping Monday
- **Pillar:** 2 (weekends).
- **Working titles:** "The Weekend Was Invented Because Nobody Showed Up on Monday" / "Workers Gave Up a Day Off to Invent the Weekend"
- **Premise:** For about 200 years, British workers simply didn't come in on Mondays (they called it "Saint Monday"), and the bosses' fix for it turned into Saturday afternoon off, and then the weekend.
- **Opening hook image:** Monday, 1790s Birmingham workshop: the boss Mr. Yellow Scarf, in a top hat, alone among silent benches, a clock ticking. A note on a bench: "Gone to church. Of Saint Monday." Outside, workers play skittles at the pub.
- **Central conflict and escalation:** Six-day week, paid on Saturday night. Craftsmen drink Saturday, rest Sunday, and take "Saint Monday" too, then cram the work into Tuesday-Saturday. Benjamin Franklin brags in his autobiography that he never took a Saint Monday in a London print shop. Factory owners hate it; church, temperance and "rational recreation" groups hate the pub. From the 1840s, the Early Closing Association proposes a trade: a Saturday afternoon off in exchange for a full Monday's work.
- **Twist:** What killed Saint Monday wasn't punishment; it was something better to do. Railways sell cheap Saturday excursions, music halls move star acts to Saturday, and in the 1890s football's Saturday 3pm kickoff hooks the working class. The Saturday half-day wins; the full two-day weekend arrives in the 20th century (Henry Ford's five-day week, 1926, in the US).
- **Payoff:** The weekend wasn't given; it was traded for a hangover day. Modern hook: today's four-day-week debate is the same fight.
- **Sources:**
  - Brad Beaven (Univ. of Portsmouth), The Conversation, "History of the two-day weekend...": https://theconversation.com/history-of-the-two-day-weekend-offers-lessons-for-todays-calls-for-a-four-day-week-127382
  - Wikipedia "Saint Monday" (cites Douglas A. Reid, "The Decline of Saint Monday 1766-1876", *Past & Present* 71, 1976, and E. P. Thompson, "Time, Work-Discipline and Industrial Capitalism", 1967; Franklin's autobiography): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Monday
  - Tribune, "How Workers Won the Weekend": https://tribunemag.co.uk/2023/03/how-workers-won-the-weekend
  - Te Ara (NZ encyclopedia), weekends: https://teara.govt.nz/en/weekends/page-2
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** (a) "Hangovers invented the weekend" is the pop simplification; historians (Beaven, Reid) say it was a workers' custom plus a negotiated trade, with drinking as one part. Frame drinking as part of the story, not the cause. (b) Reid says the decline came through leniency/negotiation more than suppression. (c) The 1867 Factory Act making the half-day mandatory appeared only in a blog result: UNVERIFIED. (d) Ford 1926 is a well-documented separate US strand; don't imply it came from Saint Monday.
- **Saturation:** MEDIUM. "Drunk people invented the weekend" has circulated (Seeker video reported by SBS, Cool Hunting, Now I Know). Exact YouTube views unknown. Our angle needs to be the trade/the twist, not "hangover fact".
- **5-minute fit:** Medium-good. No single protagonist (it's a class of workers vs bosses); needs a composite POV character (marked as dramatization) or a stand-in like Franklin as a foil. Strongest on relatability (everyone hates Mondays).
