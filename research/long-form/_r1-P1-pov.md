## 1. klondike-ton-of-goods
- **Pillar:** 1 (POV)
- **Working title:** "You Quit Your Job for the Gold Rush. Then Canada Made You Carry a Ton."
  Alternates: "POV: You Joined the Klondike Gold Rush (1897)" / "100,000 People Ran for Gold. 4,000 Found Any."
- **One-sentence premise:** It's 1897, a ship docks with Klondike gold, and you sell everything to join the rush. Then the border police tell you that you can't enter Canada without a year of food, about a ton of gear you have to carry over a mountain on your back.
- **Opening-hook image (price tag):** Mr. Yellow Scarf at the foot of the Chilkoot Pass next to a mountain of sacks, with a Mountie's clipboard: "1 YEAR OF FOOD OR GO HOME." On-screen counter: "Trips needed: 0/30+."
- **Conflict and escalation (decisions):**
  1. Choose the route: Chilkoot (steep, 1,500 ice steps, you climb it 20-40 times) or White Pass (horses, later nicknamed "Dead Horse Trail").
  2. Pay a daily fee for the cut steps, hire packers, or haul everything yourself. The April 1898 avalanche kills more than 60 people on the pass.
  3. Build a boat at Lake Bennett and run the rapids to Dawson.
  4. You arrive in 1898. Prices: eggs $3 each, nails $28/lb, champagne $60.
- **Twist:** Every good creek was staked in August 1896, before the world even heard of the strike. Close to 10,000 claims were recorded by July 1898. You crossed a mountain 30 times to reach a party that ended two years earlier.
- **Payoff / changed end state:** About 100,000 set out, 30-40k reached Dawson, 15-20k prospected, no more than 4,000 found gold and only a few hundred got rich. The people who got rich sold eggs, nails and building plots (plots went from $500 to up to $10,000-20,000). In 1899 gold turns up in Nome and Dawson empties. Mr. Yellow Scarf ends up selling his leftover flour at Dawson prices and is the only one in his group who goes home with money. That ending is a dramatization; flag it as one.
- **Sources:**
  - NPS Klondike Gold Rush NHP, "Ton of Goods": 3 lb of food per day for a year (1,095 lb, roughly doubled by gear), why Canada required it, NWMP duties of $174,000 (Feb-Jun 1898). https://home.nps.gov/klgo/learn/historyculture/tonofgoods.htm
  - Wikipedia "Klondike Gold Rush" (outcome numbers cite Pierre Berton, *Klondike*, 2001, p. 396): the 100k/30-40k/15-20k/4,000/few-hundred figures, Golden Stairs of 1,500 steps, the avalanche (3 Apr 1898, 60+ dead), claims staked by end of Aug 1896, the price list, Nome 1899. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klondike_Gold_Rush
  - Lakehead University digital exhibit, 20-40 trips over the pass: https://digitalcollections.lakeheadu.ca/exhibits/show/a-klondike-photographer---capt/starting-out
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** The trip counts vary by source (20-40 vs "about 30"). The NPS calls the "ton" a rough doubling of the required food, not a legal ton. The outcome figures are Berton's estimates. Verify the "Ton of gold" Seattle/Portland 1897 headline before using it as the hook (UNVERIFIED here). Any dollar conversions need a stated base year.
- **Saturation:** This is a known topic. Weird History's "What Was It Like To Be In The Yukon Gold Rush" (11:22) showed about 217K views in search results, and there are NFB documentaries. I found no stickman POV built on the "ton of goods" rule.
- **5-minute fit:** Good. It has 4 decision beats, a twist and a payoff. Cut the Dawson saloon tour to one beat.

## 2. whaler-greenhand-debt
- **Pillar:** 1 (POV) with a money angle (P5 flavour)
- **Working title:** "You Spent 4 Years on a Whaling Ship. You Came Home Owing Money."
  Alternates: "POV: You Signed Up for a Whaling Ship (1840s)" / "The Job That Paid 1/350th"
- **One-sentence premise:** You're a broke farm kid in 1840s New Bedford, a recruiter promises adventure and a share of the profits, and you sign for 1/350th of the catch on a voyage that could last four years.
- **Opening-hook image (price tag):** A contract on a barrel. Your share is circled: "1/350." Next to it the captain's line reads "1/8." Counter: "Voyage length: 3-4 YEARS."
- **Conflict and escalation (decisions):**
  1. You take the cash advance to sign on. It gets repaid out of your share, with interest (one source says up to 25%; verify).
  2. Life in the forecastle: "black and slimy... hot as an oven", rats and cockroaches, salt horse and "cockroach-laden molasses." A greenhand's diary line: "We have to work like horses and live like pigs."
  3. Every pair of boots and every plug of tobacco from the slop chest (the ship's overpriced store) gets charged against your share.
  4. The hunt and the processing (kept stylized, monetization-safe: no gore). Punishments: put in irons, flogging.
- **Twist:** The ship comes home full and the voyage was a success. The owner's clerk opens the ledger, takes off the advance, the interest and the slop-chest bills, and you owe *them*. An ordinary hand might earn about $25 for several years of work.
- **Payoff / changed end state:** Many men came home in debt and signed straight back on. Ending beat: Mr. Yellow Scarf stands on the dock, back where he started but owing money, and the recruiter is smiling again. Possible extra layer: the system was built so the house always won (the NBER chapter on whaling labour economics could support this; not read yet).
- **Sources:**
  - New Bedford Whaling Museum, "Life Aboard": greenhand lay as little as 1/350, captain about 1/8, advances and store purchases charged to the lay, men arriving home in debt and re-signing, $25 for several years, food and forecastle quotes, voyages of 3-4 years. https://www.whalingmuseum.org/?p=469
  - NPS New Bedford Whaling NHP, "Life onboard a Whaleship": https://www.nps.gov/nebe/learn/historyculture/whaleship.htm
  - Wikipedia "Green hand (whaling)", definition and lay range: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_hand_(whaling)
  - NEH *Humanities* 2010, "Whaling the Old Way" (Robert Weir diary quote): https://www.neh.gov/humanities/2010/marchapril/feature/whaling-the-old-way
  - Further reading: NBER chapter c8280 (Davis, Gallman, Hutchins), https://www.nber.org/chapters/c8280.pdf
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** Lay ranges vary (1/160-1/350). The figures on interest on the advance ($75, up to 25%) came from a search snippet and are UNVERIFIED. How common it was to come home in debt is not quantified. Say "many", not "most". The protagonist is composite and the story is dramatization.
- **Saturation:** I found no YouTube video on the greenhand debt angle. General "life on a whaling ship" content exists (e.g., Grunge articles), but I could not see view counts. It looks low.
- **5-minute fit:** Very good. One contract, 4 escalations and a ledger reveal.

## 3. groom-of-the-stool-norris
- **Pillar:** 1 (POV), with a royal-palace-servant direction
- **Working title:** "You Got the Worst Job in the Palace. It Made You the Second Most Powerful Man in England."
  Alternates: "POV: You're Henry VIII's Toilet Servant" / "The Toilet Job That Got You Beheaded"
- **One-sentence premise:** You get the job nobody brags about, attending Henry VIII on his close stool. Because you're the only man alone with the king, everyone in England starts bribing you, and that closeness is exactly what gets you killed.
- **Opening-hook image (clock / price tag):** Mr. Yellow Scarf in Tudor livery holding a bowl, a towel and a velvet-padded box seat, with a disgusted face. Text: "YOUR NEW JOB." Smash cut to a line of nobles holding out bags of coins to him.
- **Conflict and escalation (decisions):**
  1. The duties: supplying bowl, water and towels, monitoring the king's diet and bowel movements, and reporting to the royal doctor. Whether the groom literally wiped is debated; say so on screen, which is itself a joke.
  2. Access is power. The groom runs the Privy Chamber, which controls the king's private rooms and, under the first groom Hugh Denys, royal finances. Petitioners queue up. Do you take the bribes?
  3. Rise: Henry Norris takes the post in 1526 and becomes one of Henry's closest friends.
  4. 1536: the Anne Boleyn crisis. Norris is named.
- **Twist:** The closeness that made him powerful is what makes him a target. Norris is accused of adultery with Queen Anne and treason, refuses to confess, and is beheaded on Tower Hill on 17 May 1536. Historians widely regard the charges as false.
- **Payoff / changed end state:** You went from nobody to the king's most trusted man to the scaffold, all for being in the room. Coda: the job lasted until 1901 (as "Groom of the Stole").
- **Sources:**
  - Wikipedia "Groom of the Stool": duties, Hugh Denys, Privy Chamber, run to 1901: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groom_of_the_Stool
  - Wikipedia "Henry Norris (courtier)": appointment 1526, execution 17 May 1536: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Norris_(courtier)
  - The Tudor Society, Sir Henry Norris (cites HistoryExtra on the "bottom-wiper-in-chief" line): https://www.tudorsociety.com/sir-henry-norris-1490s-1536/
  - Historic UK, "Groom of the Stool": https://historic-uk.com/HistoryUK/HistoryofBritain/Groom-of-the-Stool/
  - Atlas Obscura, king's toilet attendant: https://atlasobscura.com/articles/king-toilet-attendant-england
  - Academic anchor to cite in fact-check: Tracy Borman, *The Private Lives of the Tudors* (2016); David Starkey's work on the Privy Chamber.
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** Whether the groom physically cleaned the king (debated). Norris's guilt (charges generally seen as fabricated; state it as "accused"). The "second most powerful" title claim is hyperbole; the critic should check whether the packaging can stand on "one of the most powerful". The bribery scenes are dramatization unless sourced specifically for Norris. Monetization: toilet humour is fine and the beheading stays off-screen.
- **Saturation:** The topic is popular as Shorts, articles and listicle segments (HELLO!, Atlas Obscura, many blogs). I could not see YouTube view counts. Expect existing explainers; the POV-to-scaffold arc through Norris looks less common.
- **5-minute fit:** Good. The risk is that the first half becomes a list of toilet facts. Keep it to 2 beats, then the Norris story.

## 4. mechanical-turk-operator
- **Pillar:** 1 (POV); could also sit in P4
- **Working title:** "Your Job: Hide Inside a 'Robot' and Beat Napoleon at Chess"
  Alternates: "POV: You're the Man Inside the Chess Robot" / "The 'Robot' That Beat Napoleon Had a Secret"
- **One-sentence premise:** You're a broke chess master, and a showman offers you a job folded inside a cabinet by candlelight, secretly playing the moves of a "thinking machine" against kings, emperors and paying crowds, where one sneeze ends the con.
- **Opening-hook image (clock):** A cross-section of the cabinet: Mr. Yellow Scarf crammed in, holding a candle, with magnets clicking above his head. Outside, the showman opens the doors one at a time and the sliding seat moves to keep him hidden. Text: "DON'T. MOVE."
- **Conflict and escalation (decisions):**
  1. Learn the tricks: a sliding seat as the doors open, magnetized pieces that pull on iron markers under the board, a pegboard and pantograph to move the Turk's arm, and brass discs for talking to the showman.
  2. 1809, Schönbrunn: Napoleon sits down and tries illegal moves. In the common account the Turk returns the piece, then takes a turn, then sweeps the board, and Napoleon then loses in 19 moves. Do you insult an emperor?
  3. America, 1826 onward: the operator is William Schlumberger, who poses as Maelzel's "secretary" when not in the box. The Turk loses some games (a Mrs. Fisher in Philadelphia, Charles Carroll in Baltimore).
  4. Baltimore heat: Schlumberger, overcome, climbs out. Two boys on a rooftop see it, and the Baltimore *Gazette* prints it.
- **Twist:** The exposé barely matters. The public mostly refuses to believe it, and some think Maelzel planted it as publicity. People want the robot to be real.
- **Payoff / changed end state:** Schlumberger dies of yellow fever in Havana in February 1838. Maelzel dies at sea months later, in debt. The Turk burns in a Philadelphia fire in 1854. Modern hook: Amazon named its human-microtask service "Mechanical Turk", so the hidden human inside the "machine" is still a business model. Verify that line as a closer.
- **Sources:**
  - Wikipedia "Mechanical Turk" (cites Standage, *The Turk*, 2002; Levitt): mechanism, Napoleon accounts, US tour, Baltimore, deaths, 1854 fire: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_Turk
  - Chess.com (batgirl), "Maelzel, Schlumberger and the Turk": https://www.chess.com/blog/batgirl/schlumberger-as-the-turk
  - JSTOR Daily, "Before Deep Blue: The Automaton Chess Player": https://daily.jstor.org/before-deep-blue-the-automaton-chess-player
  - Smithsonian, Poe and the Turk debunking: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/debunking-mechanical-turk-helped-set-edgar-allan-poe-path-mystery-writing-180964059/
  - Lost Museum Archive (CUNY): https://lostmuseum.cuny.edu/archive/automaton-chess-player-1770
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** The Napoleon game details conflict between accounts (Allen vs Mitchell; one version has Napoleon at a separate table), so frame it as "the most famous account". The candle and ventilation detail rests on one 1859 letter. The date of the Baltimore exposure is 1827 per Wikipedia (1 June 1827 Gazette) but 1828 per another source; the fact-checker must settle it. The Catherine the Great and Frederick stories are fiction; don't use them. Who operated the Turk under Kempelen is unknown. Allgaier as Napoleon's opponent-operator is "reportedly".
- **Saturation:** HIGH for the topic. Thoughty2 ("How an Unstoppable 200-Year-Old Robot Fooled the World", probably very large, count not seen), AI and Games (~10K), and many others. The POV from inside the box is the differentiator, but the critic should weigh this heavily.
- **5-minute fit:** Very good. It has natural set pieces.

## 5. ornamental-hermit-painshill
- **Pillar:** 1 (POV); the most on-brand for the lazy, sarcastic character
- **Working title:** "Rich People Paid You £700 to Do Nothing for 7 Years. You Lasted 3 Weeks."
  Alternates: "POV: You're Hired as a Rich Man's Garden Hermit" / "The Easiest Job in History (Nobody Could Do It)"
- **One-sentence premise:** In 1700s England, a rich landowner advertises for a live hermit to decorate his garden: seven years, a huge payout, but no talking, no haircuts, no leaving, and if you break a single rule you get nothing.
- **Opening-hook image (price tag + clock):** A contract nailed to a tree: "7 YEARS. £700. NO TALKING. NO HAIRCUT. NO NAIL CUTTING. NO LEAVING." Mr. Yellow Scarf signs it and grins. Counter: "Day 1 of 2,555."
- **Conflict and escalation (decisions):**
  1. The kit: a robe (accounts say camlet, goat-hair or camel-hair), a Bible, spectacles, a hassock, an hourglass, and food sent from the house.
  2. The job: visitors stroll past to gawk and you must stay silent and look holy. Some hermits elsewhere gave counsel instead (Campbell).
  3. Week by week the hair grows, the silence presses in, and the village pub is visible from the grounds.
  4. The decision: one pint.
- **Twist:** Per the legend, the Painshill hermit was found in the local pub after three weeks and dismissed, forfeiting the whole payout. Second twist: estates that couldn't find or keep a real hermit used dummies or automatons instead (verify a specific case before using).
- **Payoff / changed end state:** You end up beardless, broke and drinking in the pub, while your replacement is a stuffed dummy (if verified). Closer: the fashion died out by the 1830s and the hermit became the garden gnome, which is Campbell's subtitle "from Imperial Rome to Ornamental Gnome."
- **Sources:**
  - Smithsonian, "Ornamental Hermits Were 18th-Century England's Must-Have Garden Accessory" (contract terms, £500-700, Remington, the pub "or so the legend goes"): https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/ornamental-hermits-were-18th-century-englands-must-have-garden-accessory-180982469/ (returned 403 to fetch; content taken from the search summary)
  - Painshill (the estate itself), "The Hermitage": calls the three-week pub story a legend: https://www.painshill.co.uk/attractions/the-hermitage/
  - Country Life, "What is a garden hermit?" (camlet robe, no hair/beard/nails): https://www.countrylife.co.uk/gardens/curious-questions-what-is-a-garden-hermit-259843
  - Wikipedia "Garden hermit" (Painshill dismissal; Campbell citations): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_hermit
  - Academic: Gordon Campbell, *The Hermit in the Garden: From Imperial Rome to Ornamental Gnome* (OUP, 2013); review: https://hermitary.com/bookreviews/campbell.html
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** This is the weakest-verified candidate. The three weeks and the pub are LEGEND (Painshill says so itself). The payout is a range (£500-700, or "500 guineas"). The robe material varies. The hermit's name ("Remington") rests on secondary sources. The video must be framed openly as "the legend says", or anchored in a better-documented hermit from Campbell. The fact-checker needs Campbell's book.
- **Saturation:** I found no YouTube video in search (results were hermit crabs). Articles abound (Smithsonian, Mental Floss, Amusing Planet), so Shorts probably exist. Long-form looks LOW, but I could not confirm.
- **5-minute fit:** Borderline. The core story is thin, so it needs either Campbell's other cases (Hawkstone etc., UNVERIFIED here) or a "what did a hermit actually do" middle, which risks the rejected all-day format. The orchestrator should decide whether it is a long-form premise or a Short.
