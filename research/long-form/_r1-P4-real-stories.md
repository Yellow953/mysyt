## 1. The engineer who built his own country in the sea, and Italy blew it up
- **Pillar:** 4 (Joe's "man who tried to build his own country")
- **Working title:** "He Built His Own Country. Italy Sent the Navy."
- **One-sentence premise:** In 1968 an Italian engineer built a steel platform in the sea off Rimini, just outside Italian waters. He declared it an independent republic with its own language, stamps and government, and Italy answered with police, a naval blockade and explosives.
- **Opening-hook image:** Mr. Yellow Scarf in a hard hat on a tiny steel platform in the open sea, planting a flag. A grey warship appears on the horizon.
- **Conflict and escalation:** Giorgio Rosa, an engineer from Bologna, spends years sinking pylons into the seabed about 11 km offshore, beyond the 6-mile territorial limit of the time. He opens a bar, a restaurant and a souvenir shop, and tourists come by boat. On 1 May 1968 he declares the "Republic of Rose Island", with Esperanto as the official language and its own stamps. Rome sees tax evasion and a dangerous precedent. On 26 June 1968 police occupy the platform, and a naval blockade follows. Rosa appeals to the Council of Europe and gets nowhere.
- **Twist:** The navy can't take it apart, so they decide to blow it up. The first charges fail and the island is still standing. It takes another bombing, and then a storm finishes it off. The only "war" Italy declared after WWII (a popular line, UNVERIFIED as a formal claim; do not use without a source) was against one engineer's platform.
- **Payoff:** Rosa's "government in exile" issues stamps showing the destruction of his island. He goes back to being an engineer, lives to 92 (died 2017), and in 2009 divers find the ruins on the seabed. The ending is bittersweet and has a clean shape: a dream, a fight, a loss, a legacy.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Rose_Island ; https://www.ilpost.it/2020/12/09/isola-delle-rose-storia-vera/ (Il Post, Italian journalism) ; https://www.wantedinrome.com/news/rose-island-a-true-and-incredible-italian-story.html ; https://interestingengineering.com/innovation/giorgio-rosa-the-engineer-who-built-his-own-island
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** The demolition date varies by source (22 Jan, 11 Feb, 13 Feb or 26 Feb 1969). The "only war Italy ever declared" line is folklore, so drop it or label it. Officials accused him of a tax and tourism money scheme, while he said it was about freedom. Present both sides. The 2020 Netflix film (Rose Island) romanticizes the ending, so don't pull details from it.
- **Saturation:** MEDIUM. The 2020 Netflix film raised awareness, especially in Italy, and there's an Italian documentary. I couldn't find view counts for English YouTube explainers. Micronation compilations usually mention it briefly, but a full narrated story seems less common than Sealand. Verify in Chrome.
- **5-minute fit:** Good. Five clear acts (build, declare, occupy, bomb, legacy). Rosa's personality is thin in English sources, so the Il Post piece is needed for human detail.

## 2. The butcher who came home as a baronet, and his "mother" believed him
- **Pillar:** 4 (scandal that sounds fictional; obsession and betrayal)
- **Working title:** "A Butcher Claimed to Be a Lost Aristocrat. His 'Mother' Said Yes."
- **One-sentence premise:** In 1866 a heavy-set butcher in an Australian bush town claimed to be Roger Tichborne, the slim, French-speaking heir to a fortune who had been lost at sea. Roger's grieving mother looked at him and declared he was her son.
- **Opening-hook image:** A thin aristocrat's portrait next to a huge sweaty butcher in an apron. Mr. Yellow Scarf as the butcher, holding a cleaver, says "Hi, Mum."
- **Conflict and escalation:** Roger Tichborne vanished in a shipwreck in 1854. His mother refused to accept it and ran newspaper ads around the world. In Wagga Wagga, "Thomas Castro", a butcher, answers. He comes to England and is accepted by Lady Tichborne, who gives him money. The rest of the family calls him a fraud. Roger spoke French as his first language, and the claimant can't. A huge civil trial follows (1871-72, around 100 witnesses for him), and he loses. He is then tried for perjury and gets 14 years' hard labour.
- **Twist:** The jury finds he is Arthur Orton, a butcher's son from Wapping in London. Even so, the working-class public turns him into a folk hero, an underdog fighting the establishment. Mass rallies and a political movement grow up around a man the courts call a liar.
- **Payoff:** He's released in 1884, tours music halls and circuses, and in 1895 sells a confession to a newspaper, then retracts it. He dies poor in 1898, and his coffin plate reads "Sir Roger Charles Doughty Tichborne." Nobody fully knows what the mother truly believed.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tichborne_case ; https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/orton-arthur-4341 (Australian Dictionary of Biography) ; https://www2.sl.nsw.gov.au/archive/discover_collections/history_nation/justice/felons/tichborne/index.html (State Library of NSW) ; https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography,_1901_supplement/Orton,_Arthur
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** Most historians accept that he was Orton, but a minority think some doubt remains. The 1895 confession was retracted. Trial lengths vary between sources. The coffin-plate detail is widely reported but needs one more check (UNVERIFIED here). Lady Tichborne died in 1868 before the trials; check her exact role and timeline.
- **Saturation:** LOW-MEDIUM. There's a 1998 film and several podcasts, but I couldn't find a big YouTube video. History-buff channels have probably covered it, but it's not a list staple. Verify in Chrome.
- **5-minute fit:** Good, but the courtroom part is dense. Compress the trials into one montage and keep the mother-son recognition as the emotional center. This one has the strongest protagonist the viewer can care about (hate-love).

## 3. The professor whose jealous colleagues buried fake fossils for him, and he wrote a book about them
- **Pillar:** 4 (revenge / jealousy / humiliation)
- **Working title:** "His Rivals Faked Fossils to Humiliate Him. He Wrote a Whole Book About Them."
- **One-sentence premise:** In 1725 a proud professor in Würzburg started receiving "fossils" of frogs, spiders, comets and stones carved with the name of God in Hebrew. They had been carved and planted by colleagues who wanted to ruin him, and he published them as a scientific discovery.
- **Opening-hook image:** Mr. Yellow Scarf in a powdered wig, triumphantly holding up a stone with a perfect little spider on it. Behind a tree, two men are snickering.
- **Conflict and escalation:** Johann Beringer, a medicine professor and the prince-bishop's physician, pays local teens to dig on Mount Eibelstadt. The finds get more and more absurd: frogs mating, birds, the sun and moon, then Hebrew and Latin letters. He doesn't get suspicious. He gets more excited. Each absurd find gets past the scientific doubts of the time, and he writes a lavish illustrated book (Lithographiae Wirceburgensis, 1726).
- **Twist:** The fossils were carved by colleagues, the geography professor J. Ignatz Roderick and the librarian Johann Georg von Eckhart, reportedly because they found Beringer arrogant. Beringer himself takes them to court, and their secret comes out in testimony.
- **Payoff:** The hoaxers are disgraced: Roderick leaves Würzburg, and Eckhart loses his library privileges. Beringer, humiliated, reportedly tries to buy back every copy of his book. Centuries later, the "Lying Stones" are museum pieces and the classic example of a scientist seeing what he wanted to see.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Beringer ; https://depositsmag.com/2018/03/29/fake-fossils-by-the-hundred-the-story-of-johann-beringers-lying-stones/ (Paul Taylor, Deposits) ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/history-of-geology/frauds-fakes-and-fossils ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/5-of-the-greatest-natural-history-hoaxes-of-all-time/ (Stephen Jay Gould also wrote about it in "The Lying Stones of Marrakech", which we could cite if we need a book source)
- **Disputed / exaggerated (important):** The revenge/jealousy motive is the popular version and is NOT settled. A Scientific American history-of-geology blog argues that Roderick arrived only in winter 1725-26, after the first stones, and that neither man had a clear motive, so the origin of the first stones is unresolved. Taylor (Deposits) says "the truth is still shrouded in mystery." The "bankrupted himself buying back the book" claim is popular legend (UNVERIFIED). One story about him finding his own name carved on a stone is probably apocryphal (UNVERIFIED, do not use). Safest framing: "court records say these two were behind it, but the mystery isn't fully solved."
- **Saturation:** LOW-MEDIUM. It appears in hoax lists and as a short segment. I didn't find a dedicated big video (no counts available).
- **5-minute fit:** Good. The escalation of ever-crazier fossils is visual comedy gold for stickman art. The court ending is a little legalistic, so keep it short.

## 4. The fake surgeon who operated on a warship, and saved everyone
- **Pillar:** 4 (scandal that sounds fictional)
- **Working title:** "He Faked Being a Surgeon. Then a Warship Needed Surgery."
- **One-sentence premise:** In 1951 an American high-school dropout borrowed a real doctor's identity, joined the Royal Canadian Navy as a surgeon, and in the middle of the Korean War had to operate on wounded men after reading a textbook overnight.
- **Opening-hook image:** Mr. Yellow Scarf in a surgeon's mask, a surgery textbook open next to him, a patient on the table, sweating as the ship rocks.
- **Conflict and escalation:** Ferdinand Waldo Demara Jr. has already been a fake monk, teacher and psychologist. He meets a young doctor, Joseph Cyr, borrows his credentials, and joins HMCS Cayuga as ship's doctor. He bluffs through ordinary medicine, and then a boat arrives carrying wounded Korean soldiers. He is the only "surgeon."
- **Twist:** He operates, reportedly removing a bullet from near a man's heart, and nobody dies. The navy is so proud it publicizes his heroics. The real Dr. Cyr's family reads the newspaper story and recognizes the name. His success is what exposes him.
- **Payoff:** Canada quietly sends him home rather than prosecuting. He sells his story to LIFE, it becomes the film The Great Impostor (Tony Curtis, 1961), and he keeps impersonating people. Late in life he reportedly works as a real hospital chaplain. The question for the viewer: was he a con man or a natural?
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Waldo_Demara ; https://legionmagazine.com/playing-doctor-aboard-ship/ (Legion Magazine, Canadian military history) ; https://content.time.com/time/subscriber/article/0,33009,889381,00.html (TIME 1952, "Medicine: All at Sea") ; https://ncronline.org/news/people/brother-doctor-soldier-lies
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** The number of surgeries varies (some say 16, others "several") and so do the details of what he operated on. Much of the colorful detail comes from Demara himself via Robert Crichton's 1959 biography, so he is an unreliable narrator. "Textbook overnight" is reported but rests partly on his own account. Navy records were not checked.
- **Saturation:** MEDIUM. He's a known "great impostor", with some YouTube coverage and podcasts, but I didn't find a dominant video (no counts). Probably mentioned in impostor lists. Verify in Chrome.
- **5-minute fit:** Very good if we focus only on the Cayuga episode (with a 30-second montage of his earlier fake careers as escalation). It's a premise that needs no explanation.

## 5. The servant girl who invented a country and became a princess
- **Pillar:** 4 (scandal / hoax that sounds fictional)
- **Working title:** "A Servant Girl Invented a Kingdom. England Treated Her Like Royalty."
- **One-sentence premise:** In 1817 a young woman in a turban walked into an English village speaking a language nobody understood. Within weeks the local gentry believed she was Princess Caraboo of "Javasu", kidnapped by pirates, when she was really Mary Baker, a cobbler's daughter from Devon.
- **Opening-hook image:** A muddy village lane, a woman in an exotic turban speaking gibberish, and a crowd of villagers in top hats leaning in, fascinated. Mr. Yellow Scarf as the confused cobbler who found her.
- **Conflict and escalation:** A cobbler in Almondsbury, near Bristol, finds her and she's taken to the magistrate Samuel Worrall, whose wife takes her in. A "Portuguese sailor" claims to understand her language and "translates" a tale of pirates, a royal father and a daring escape. She fences, swims naked in the lake, prays to "Allah-Tallah" and climbs on the roof. The more she performs, the more people believe her, and scholars and society visitors arrive to study the princess.
- **Twist:** A Bristol newspaper prints her portrait, and her former landlady (Mrs. Neale) recognizes her. The princess suddenly speaks perfect English. The "language" was improvised gibberish.
- **Payoff:** Instead of punishing her, Mrs. Worrall pays for her passage to Philadelphia. She returns years later, tries to exhibit herself as Caraboo with no success, and ends her life selling leeches to Bristol hospitals. She never explained why she did it.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princess_Caraboo ; https://hoaxes.org/archive/permalink/princess_caraboo (Museum of Hoaxes) ; https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/92250/mysterious-19th-century-princess-who-fooled-town-thinking-she-was-royalty ; https://blog.newspapers.com/clever-cons-princess-caraboo/ (period newspaper scans) ; https://orlando.cambridge.org/people/be56e0c2-a062-4052-a3aa-be8205d77d09 (Cambridge Orlando)
- **Disputed / exaggerated:** Her birth year is 1791 or 1792. The scene where Mrs. Neale exposes her is told with flourishes that some sources call embellished. The Portuguese "translator" is reported in some accounts but his role is murky. The "swam naked" detail is in period accounts. It's monetization-safe if handled as a brief cheeky line without imagery. The 1994 film (Phoebe Cates) adds fictional incidents, so don't use the film.
- **Saturation:** LOW-MEDIUM. There's a 1994 film and many blog articles, but I found no big YouTube video (no counts).
- **5-minute fit:** Good. It's fun and escalating, with a female protagonist who has wit. The weakness is a soft ending (no big consequence), and the payoff rests on the leech seller and the mystery of her motive.
