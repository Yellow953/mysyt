# Story hunt H3: impossible situations and human conflict

Hunter: H3 · Date: 2026-11-03 · Seam: cons, impostors, heists, rivalries, ticking clocks, ordinary people in bizarre real situations.

**How YouTube saturation was checked:** a read-only YouTube search in Claude in Chrome for each story (one or two queries each), on 2026-11-03. Results are personalised and the page showed only the first screen, so the counts are a rough signal, not a census. **Pattern across ALL eight stories:** in the last 1 to 3 months, dozens of tiny AI-narrated channels (mostly under 1K views, many uploaded "1d" or "2w ago") have been mining the same "stranger than fiction" lists. No story here is untouched. The useful question is whether a **big** video already exists. Where none does, a well-packaged video can still win.

No scores, as requested. Dropped after checking: Lincoln Hall (left for dead on Everest, 2006), because it is saturated (I Shouldn't Be Alive episode at 731K, another at 255K, more at 50K to 77K) and it is too grim for a sarcastic mascot. Also dropped: Trebitsch-Lincoln (Hungarian con man who became a British MP, then a German spy, then a Buddhist abbot in Shanghai). It is a great "wait, WHAT?", but it covers 30 years across four countries, so it cannot fit 5 minutes. Keep it for a longer format. Main source: Bernard Wasserstein, *The Secret Life of Trebitsch Lincoln*; see also https://www.historytoday.com/archive/chasing-chameleon-trebitsch-lincoln

## 1. The Shoemaker Who Arrested a Mayor (Captain of Köpenick, Germany, 1906)

- **One-sentence pitch:** A broke ex-convict shoemaker bought a used army captain's uniform piece by piece, ordered real soldiers off the street, marched them into a town hall, arrested the mayor and walked off with the treasury. Then the Kaiser laughed and pardoned him.
- **Opening-hook image:** Mr. Yellow Scarf in a mismatched second-hand Prussian uniform, a little too big for him, barking orders at a squad of real soldiers who salute without question.
- **Central conflict and escalation:** Voigt is trapped by bureaucracy. With no papers he cannot work, and with no work he cannot get papers. He had spent about 25 years of his life in prison, and in August 1906 the police expelled him from Berlin. The uniform test works, so he goes bigger. First 4 grenadiers and a sergeant on the street, then 6 more soldiers from a shooting range. He takes a train to Köpenick, has the exits sealed, orders the police to keep "law and order", and blocks phone calls to Berlin for an hour. He arrests the mayor and the treasurer for "corruption", the officials hand over the cash, and he leaves the soldiers standing guard for another half hour while he changes clothes and vanishes.
- **Biggest twist:** Reportedly he did it for a passport, but the passport office was not in that building (per HistoryNet; Wikipedia does not confirm the passport motive, see disputes). Also, he signed the receipt for the money with the name of his former prison warden (Wikipedia).
- **Ending/payoff:** A former cellmate tips off the police for the reward. He is caught 10 days later and gets 4 years. The public finds it hilarious, and Kaiser Wilhelm II pardons him in 1908 after about 20 months. Four days after release he has a waxwork of himself in a Berlin museum. He tours variety shows as "the Captain of Köpenick", sells postcards of himself, writes a book (1909), and dies in Luxembourg in 1922. During WWI the occupying Germans arrested him for wearing a uniform without authorisation. Perfect sarcastic closer: the uniform was the one thing he was never allowed to keep.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Voigt
  - https://www.historynet.com/captain-of-kopenick/
  - https://hoaxes.org/archive/permalink/the_captain_of_koepenick
  - https://www.irishtimes.com/news/from-cobbler-to-celebrity-by-donning-a-captain-s-uniform-1.1015108
- **Disputed or legendary:** The amount varies by source (4,000 / 3,557.45 / 4,002.37 marks). Use "about 4,000 marks". The Kaiser "laughing out loud" is "reportedly" and comes mainly from one popular history. The passport motive appears in HistoryNet and the plays but not in Wikipedia's factual section, so treat it as "reportedly". The detail that a French platoon saluted his funeral, mistaking it for an officer's, comes only from HistoryNet and is UNVERIFIED, so do not use it as fact. Whether the mayor was actually corrupt is unknown (probably not).
- **YouTube saturation:** MEDIUM. One recent large video (82K, 4 months old). Older ones: Plainly Difficult (28K, 9 years old) and House of History (6.1K). The full 1956 film has 748K views. There are about 20 small Shorts and videos (most under 1.5K), several uploaded in the last few days. No dominant English story video.
- **Needs more than 5 minutes?** No. It is a single day plus an aftermath, which fits 5 minutes well.

## 2. The Man Who Ordered Real Money (Alves dos Reis, Portugal, 1925)

- **One-sentence pitch:** A Portuguese con man never faked a single banknote. He faked a contract and got the central bank's own official printer in London to print him 100 million escudos in REAL money, about 1% of Portugal's GDP.
- **Opening-hook image:** Two identical banknotes side by side, same serial number, and nobody can say which one is fake, because neither is.
- **Central conflict and escalation:** He starts small and keeps doubling down. He forges an engineering diploma from an Oxford school that did not exist, and uses forged cheques to buy a railway company and then loot its treasury to cover them. He is jailed for 54 days and designs the big scheme inside his cell. The forged Bank of Portugal contract has forged governors' signatures, a careless notary's stamp, and certification by British, German and French embassies. A Dutch front man takes it to Waterlow & Sons, who print 200,000 notes of 500 escudos from the bank's real plates. Reis opens his own bank to launder them, then starts buying up Bank of Portugal shares (10,000 of the 45,000 he needs) so he can take over the bank and make the fraud retroactively legal.
- **Biggest twist:** He gets caught because the notes are too real. Waterlow reused serial numbers the bank had already issued, and a bank inspector finds duplicate serials in Porto. Second twist: in 1932 the House of Lords made the PRINTER pay (£610,392). Waterlow never fully recovered.
- **Ending/payoff:** Exposed on 5 December 1925, arrested on returning to Lisbon, sentenced to 20 years in 1930. He converts to Protestantism in prison, gets out in 1945 and dies in 1955. The crisis is often linked to the May 1926 coup that led to the Salazar dictatorship.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alves_dos_Reis
  - https://www.bhsportugal.org/library/articles/alves-reis-and-the-portuguese-bank-note-scandal-of-1925 (Andrew Bull, British Historical Society of Portugal, 1997)
  - https://insider.spink.com/?p=2694 (Spink, numismatic dealer)
  - Book: Murray Teigh Bloom, *The Man Who Stole Portugal* (1966). Known standard account, not opened in this session.
- **Disputed or legendary:** Some sources say 1% of GDP, Wikipedia's body says 0.88%. Bull gives about 16.5% of notes in circulation. The link to the 1926 coup is contested and described as "little understood", so say "some historians argue". Whether his associates (Marang, Hennies, Bandeira) knew is disputed. The rival newspaper O Século had business motives for digging, which is a nice detail but should be told as "rival".
- **YouTube saturation:** LOW to MEDIUM. About 20 results, almost all tiny (most under 750 views), many from the last 2 weeks. The largest is about 1.2K, a Short. No big English video found on this query. A film adaptation is reportedly in development (theportugalnews.com, May 2026), so interest may rise.
- **Needs more than 5 minutes?** It is tight in 5 minutes. Cut the Ambaca railway backstory to one line and focus on contract, printer, real money, duplicate serials, and the printer paying.

## 3. The Princess From an Island That Doesn't Exist (Princess Caraboo, England, 1817)

- **One-sentence pitch:** A homeless cobbler's daughter turned up in an English village speaking a language she had invented, and for ten weeks the local gentry treated her as a kidnapped princess from the island of "Javasu", with a sailor "translating" for her.
- **Opening-hook image:** A barefoot young woman in a turban, fencing with a stick on a country-house lawn while well-dressed gentlemen take notes.
- **Central conflict and escalation:** The magistrate Samuel Worrall thinks she is a beggar and has her held for vagrancy. Then a Portuguese sailor, Manuel Eynesso, claims he understands her. Her story: pirates kidnapped her, and she jumped overboard in the Bristol Channel and swam ashore. Each audience asks for more, and she delivers more. She shoots a bow, fences, swims naked (keep it cheeky and off-screen), and prays to "Allah-Talla". A Dr Wilkinson vouches for her language, and the newspapers print her portrait.
- **Biggest twist:** The portrait is what sinks her. Mrs Neale, a boarding-house keeper who had housed her, recognises the face in the paper. She is Mary Willcocks (later Baker) from Devon, and the "oriental surgery" marks on her head were cupping scars from a London poorhouse.
- **Ending/payoff:** Mrs Worrall forgives her and pays her passage to Philadelphia (June 1817). In America she performs as the Princess with little success, comes back, and ends up selling leeches to the Bristol Infirmary (1839). The worst deal ever: from royalty to leeches.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princess_Caraboo
  - https://hoaxes.org/archive/permalink/princess_caraboo (Museum of Hoaxes)
  - https://www.mentalfloss.com/history/mystery/mysterious-19th-century-princess-who-fooled-town-thinking-she-was-royalty
  - https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Devonshire_Characters_and_Strange_Events/Caraboo (Baring-Gould, 1908)
- **Disputed or legendary:** The claim that Napoleon wanted to marry her is a later newspaper joke, so do NOT use it as fact. Her birth year (1791 or 1792) and year of death (1864 or 1865) are uncertain. The sailor may have been an accomplice or a genuine believer; sources differ. The 1994 film added fictional incidents.
- **YouTube saturation:** MEDIUM. Film trailers (75K). Story videos: Forgotten Lives (33K, 3 years old), After Dark (25K, 5 months old), The Folklorist (21K). One Short has 56K. Plenty of small ones.
- **Needs more than 5 minutes?** No.

## 4. The Pensioner Who Kidnapped the Duke of Wellington (Kempton Bunton, London, 1961)

- **One-sentence pitch:** A 61-year-old, 17-stone retired bus driver "kidnapped" a Goya from the National Gallery and held it for ransom for four years to pay for pensioners' TV licences. Decades later, a sealed file revealed his son had actually taken it.
- **Opening-hook image:** An empty frame-shaped patch on a National Gallery wall. Cut to the same painting hanging in Dr. No's lair in the 1962 Bond film, because the public assumed a master criminal had it.
- **Central conflict and escalation:** One man against the TV licence fee. He had already been jailed several times for refusing to pay. The government had just bought the Goya for £140,000 to keep it in Britain, the same sum he demands go to charity for pensioners' licences. He sends five ransom letters (to Reuters, then the Telegraph Exchange) while Scotland Yard hunts an international art gang. Four years pass, and the painting turns up in a left-luggage office at Birmingham New Street.
- **Biggest twist:** First twist: six weeks after returning it, he walks into a police station and confesses. Second: the jury acquits him of stealing the painting, because he never meant to keep it, and convicts him only of stealing the FRAME, which was never found. He gets 3 months, and Parliament changes the law (Theft Act 1968, s.11). Third, the big one: in 1969 his son John confessed to the actual theft after an unrelated arrest. The file stayed secret until 2012, and nobody was ever prosecuted.
- **Ending/payoff:** The old man went to prison for a frame to cover for his son. Strong emotional and sarcastic ending.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/research/research-centre/archive/record/NGA26 (National Gallery archive record)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kempton_Bunton
  - https://blog.nationalarchives.gov.uk/the-amazing-story-behind-the-duke/ (National Archives; it moved to the web archive and I could not fetch the full text, the summary came from search results)
  - https://www.scotsman.com/arts-and-culture/film-and-tv/the-duke-what-happened-to-kempton-bunton-and-is-the-duke-a-true-story-3581162
- **Disputed or legendary:** Bunton's own account of the theft (a loosened toilet window, the alarm off during cleaning) is unverified. Why John confessed varies by source (fingerprint fear or arrest in a stolen car). Whether Bunton really intended to return the painting is disputed. Wikipedia's claim that the ransom was declined is unsourced. The 2020 film *The Duke* renamed John "Jackie" and dramatised events.
- **YouTube saturation:** MEDIUM and rising. Lume posted a 132K-view video 3 months ago that covers the son twist, which is the main competitor. The rest are small (under 1.3K). The film raised awareness in 2022.
- **Needs more than 5 minutes?** No. It fits 5 minutes cleanly with three reveals.

## 5. The 30-Year-Old Schoolboy (Brian MacKinnon / "Brandon Lee", Scotland, 1993-95)

- **One-sentence pitch:** A 30-year-old man who had failed out of medical school went back to his OWN old high school pretending to be a 16-year-old Canadian, got straight A's, starred in the school musical, and got into medical school again.
- **Opening-hook image:** A school class photo. Every kid is 16 except one, who has a receding hairline and is smiling as if nothing is wrong.
- **Central conflict and escalation:** One goal: become a doctor. He attended Bearsden Academy 1974-80, failed exams in medicine at Glasgow, and worked as a janitor at a health club. In 1993 he re-enrols at the same school as "Brandon Lee". His cover story is a Canadian whose opera-singer mother died in a car crash, now living with his grandmother. Near-misses keep building: his 1970s teachers are still on the staff, he gets spotted in town, and he drives classmates around ("in Canada you can drive at 14"). He stars in the school's *South Pacific* production opposite a 15-year-old classmate (non-romantic framing only), then gets five A grades and a place in medicine at Dundee.
- **Biggest twist:** He made it. The deception worked all the way to the goal, and then an anonymous phone call to the education authorities and the newspapers ended it (1995).
- **Ending/payoff:** Expelled from Dundee. Years later a former classmate, Jono McLeod, made the documentary *My Old School* (2022). MacKinnon would only give audio, so Alan Cumming lip-syncs his voice on screen. Cumming had been attached to play him in a 1990s biopic called *Younger Than Springtime*, a song from *South Pacific*, which was never made.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_MacKinnon
  - https://news.stv.tv/feature/glasgow-schoolboy-brandon-lee-turned-out-to-be-30-year-old-man-brian-mackinnon (STV News)
  - https://www.thecourier.co.uk/fp/news/dundee/4023529/brian-mackinnon-brandon-lee-dundee-university-my-old-school/ (The Courier)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/My_Old_School_(2022_film)
- **Disputed or legendary:** His age is given as 30, 31 or 32, and his claimed age as 16 or 17. Who made the anonymous call is unknown. One YouTube title says he did it "twice", and a Press and Journal headline mentions an "Inverurie Brandon Lee". UNVERIFIED (paywalled/403), so do not use it. Careful framing is needed: an adult among teenagers. Keep it about deception and ambition, nothing romantic. The *South Pacific* co-star was 15, so mention only that he "starred in the school musical".
- **YouTube saturation:** MEDIUM to HIGH recently. Carly Incontro's video has 217K views and is 3 weeks old (a big fresh competitor). The documentary trailers have 192K, 23K and 16K. Small explainers have about 1K. Note that YouTube search for "Brandon Lee" is flooded by the actor, which hurts discoverability but also makes a clear title like "The 30-Year-Old Who Went Back to High School" stand out.
- **Needs more than 5 minutes?** No. Risk: a recent 217K video and a feature documentary.

## 6. The MP Who Faked His Death and Got Mistaken for a Murderer (John Stonehouse, UK/USA/Australia, 1974)

- **One-sentence pitch:** A former British cabinet minister faked his drowning on a Miami beach to escape his debts, started a new life in Australia under a dead man's name, and was caught only because police thought he was a completely different missing aristocrat.
- **Opening-hook image:** A neat pile of clothes on a Miami beach next to a cabana, while the man who left them is boarding a plane.
- **Central conflict and escalation:** His businesses are collapsing and the Department of Trade is investigating. He rehearses the identities of dead constituents' husbands (Joseph Markham, then "Clive Mildoon"). On 20 November 1974 he leaves his clothes with a Fontainebleau Hotel cabana attendant and disappears, and the world mourns him. In Australia he moves large sums between banks under fake names, which makes a bank teller suspicious.
- **Biggest twist:** Australian police put him under surveillance because they think he is Lord Lucan, who had vanished two weeks earlier after his children's nanny was killed. They check for Lucan's known thigh scar (Stonehouse had none). They arrest him on Christmas Eve 1974 to find out who he actually is. Second twist: back in the UK and out on bail, he KEPT HIS SEAT and gave a Commons speech blaming a "parallel personality".
- **Ending/payoff:** A 68-day trial in which he defended himself. Convicted on fraud counts and sentenced to 7 years (1976). Released in 1979 after heart attacks, he married his secretary Sheila Buckley and wrote novels. Coda: a 2009 MI5 history backed the claim that he had been a Czechoslovak spy all along.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Stonehouse
  - https://crimeandinvestigation.co.uk/articles/john-stonehouse-politician-who-spied-government-and-faked-his-own-death
  - https://airmail.news/issues/2021-8-7/stone-cold-liar
  - Book: Julian Hayes, *Stonehouse: Cabinet Minister, Fraudster, Spy* (2021). Cited by Wikipedia, not opened.
- **Disputed or legendary:** Some details of the spy allegation remain disputed, and his daughter Julia contests them. The "breakdown" defence was not accepted. Small YouTube titles claiming a "library book fingerprint" or "receipt" caught him are not supported by my sources. The account I verified is a teller's suspicion, then surveillance under the Lucan theory. The Lucan backstory involves a murder, so mention it in one non-graphic line.
- **YouTube saturation:** MEDIUM. A Grain of Salt has 44K (1 year old). Archive pieces: Thames TV (11K), Open Media (13K), NewsReel (6.7K). ITV aired a drama in 2023 (*Stonehouse*). Many tiny Shorts.
- **Needs more than 5 minutes?** Borderline. The spy layer is optional. Without it, 5 minutes works.

## 7. The Will That Turned Toronto Into a Baby Race (Great Stork Derby, Canada, 1926-36)

- **One-sentence pitch:** A prankster lawyer died and left his fortune to whichever Toronto woman had the most babies in the next ten years. Families really competed, through the Great Depression, all the way to the Supreme Court.
- **Opening-hook image:** A racetrack starting line, except the runners are pregnant mothers pushing prams, and a giant 10-year countdown clock is ticking.
- **Central conflict and escalation:** Charles Vance Millar, a bachelor and practical joker, wrote a will full of traps. Brewery shares went to teetotal ministers. Jockey Club shares went to anti-gambling campaigners. A Jamaica holiday home went to three men who hated each other, on condition they share it. The ninth clause is the baby race. A worthless-looking stake in the Detroit-Windsor tunnel booms when the tunnel opens in 1930, and the pot grows to nearly a million dollars. The Depression makes it life or death for poor families. The Ontario government tries to legislate it away and fails. The press keeps a running scoreboard ("Mrs. Kenny Leads in Stork Derby", 1936).
- **Biggest twist:** The front-runners lose on technicalities. The court counts only children who are legitimate, alive and registered. One mother's 10 children include 5 conceived out of wedlock. Another's children who died in infancy don't count. Grace Bagnato is reportedly excluded over her husband's immigration status.
- **Ending/payoff:** Four mothers tie at 9 babies each and split the fortune. Three of them have to repay the city relief money they had received. Two disqualified mothers get $12,500 each to drop their appeals. Nobody knows if Millar meant it or was laughing at lawyers from beyond the grave.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Stork_Derby
  - https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/charles-vance-millar (The Canadian Encyclopedia)
  - https://torontohistory.substack.com/p/the-great-toronto-stork-derby (Adam Bunch, from *The Toronto Book of Love*)
  - Book: Elizabeth Wilton, *Bearing the Burden: The Great Toronto Stork Derby 1926-1938*. Not opened.
- **Disputed or legendary:** The payout is $110,000 each (Wikipedia) or $125,000 (Bunch). The estate size is given as $500K, $750K or "nearly a million". One winner's name has a "failed verification" tag on Wikipedia (Timleck). The Bagnato reason comes from one source only. Bunch says a second mayor-linked contest was revived in 1945, which is UNVERIFIED. Tone: keep it cheeky. It is about money and courts, not sex.
- **YouTube saturation:** MEDIUM. History & Hearsay has 80K (1 year old). House of History has 4.7K. Lots of tiny Shorts. It is fairly well known in Canada.
- **Needs more than 5 minutes?** No. The will clauses make a great 30-second cold open.

## 8. The Diamond Field That Was Salted With a Toothpick (Great Diamond Hoax, USA, 1872)

- **One-sentence pitch:** Two Kentucky cousins bought cheap gems in London, scattered them on a remote mesa, and fooled Tiffany's, a bank, two generals and Horace Greeley into a diamond rush worth millions, until a young government geologist went looking.
- **Opening-hook image:** A blindfolded party of top-hatted millionaires on horseback, being led in circles through the Wyoming wilderness by two scruffy prospectors.
- **Central conflict and escalation:** In 1870 they show a bag of rough diamonds to a San Francisco financier and swear him to secrecy. He immediately tells banker William Ralston. To look reluctant, the cousins demand a buyout and get $50,000. Then Charles Tiffany himself values a sample at $150,000, with General McClellan, Benjamin Butler and Horace Greeley in the room. A hired mining engineer (Janin) is taken to the site blindfolded, finds gems, and stakes 3,000 acres. A company is formed and the cousins walk away with about $650,000.
- **Biggest twist:** Clarence King's Fortieth Parallel Survey had mapped that whole region and knew there were no diamonds. King goes himself (October 1872) and finds rubies, sapphires and diamonds lying together, which nature never does. He finds gems placed in holes at the bottom of anthills, and nothing below the surface. He rides hard to warn the investors before more shares are sold. The New York Times writes that "shrewd" San Francisco capitalists were fooled.
- **Ending/payoff:** Exposed on 26 November 1872. Arnold returns to Kentucky with most of the money, settles the lawsuits, opens a bank, and is shot in 1878 in a quarrel with a rival banker (non-graphic: he dies months later of pneumonia). Slack vanishes and reportedly dies in New Mexico in 1896. King goes on to become the first director of the US Geological Survey.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.wyohistory.org/encyclopedia/diamond-hoax-bonanza-never-was (WyoHistory.org, Wyoming State Historical Society)
  - https://www.earthmagazine.org/article/november-261872-great-diamond-hoax-exposed/ (EARTH Magazine, American Geosciences Institute)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Arnold
- **Disputed or legendary:** Was Arnold "forced to return" the money (EARTH) or did he settle out of court (WyoHistory)? Say "settled". EARTH's "12 rubies per diamond" ratio is reported by one source only. The "tool marks on the diamonds" claim is unverified, so do not use it. Ralston's death in 1875 is often called a suicide but is debated; leave it out. Accounts differ on how King became suspicious (a train meeting with Janin, or Emmons' report). The title's "toothpick" is a stylistic flourish, not a sourced fact; change it if it would be read as a claim.
- **YouTube saturation:** LOW to MEDIUM. The Gemstones channel has 38K (5 years old). Everything else is under 900 views, mostly brand-new AI uploads.
- **Needs more than 5 minutes?** No.
